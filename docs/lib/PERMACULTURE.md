## Attribution & Ownership

**Permaculture is a design system co-originated by Bill Mollison (1928–2016) and David Holmgren (b. 1955).** Mollison was Senior Lecturer in Environmental Psychology at the University of Tasmania and a recipient of the Right Livelihood Award (1981). Holmgren, his graduate student, co-authored the foundational text *Permaculture One* (1978) and subsequently articulated the system's theoretical principles in *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability* (2002).

The term "permaculture" was coined in 1978 as a contraction of "permanent agriculture" — later expanded to "permanent culture" as the design system's scope grew from food production to the full pattern of sustainable human settlement: shelter, energy, water, community, governance.

## §1. The Core Question

> *"Though the problems of the world are increasingly complex, the solutions remain embarrassingly simple."*
> — Bill Mollison

Mollison and Holmgren observed that:
- Industrial agriculture and settlement patterns degrade the ecological systems they depend upon
- Indigenous and traditional cultures maintained productive landscapes for millennia through observation-based, place-adapted design
- The missing element in modern sustainability is not knowledge of individual techniques but a **design methodology** for integrating them — arranging elements so their relationships generate emergent yields
- Ethics must precede design: without an axiological foundation, technique becomes extractive regardless of intent

The core question is not "what should we grow?" but **"how do we design systems in which the relationships between elements generate more than the sum of the parts?"**

This reframes the designer's task: not imposing order on a site, but observing the patterns already present and arranging elements to amplify beneficial relationships. The designer reads the landscape before drawing on it.

## §2. The Ethics Triad

The ethics triad is the axiological foundation. All design flows from these three commitments; no principle or technique is valid without them.

| Ethic | Commitment | Design Expression |
|-------|------------|-------------------|
| **Earth Care** | Stewardship of living systems; provision for all life systems to continue and multiply | Design that leaves ecological systems healthier than it found them |
| **People Care** | Meeting human needs without exploitation; provision for people to access resources necessary for their existence | Design that serves human wellbeing without externalising costs to others |
| **Fair Share** | Setting limits to consumption; redistributing surplus to serve the first two ethics | Design that produces yields and shares them — no unlimited accumulation |

**The ethics are sequential and nested:** Earth Care is the container (without functioning ecosystems, nothing else is possible). People Care operates within that container. Fair Share is the feedback mechanism that prevents People Care from overrunning Earth Care.

### Transposability

The triad transposes beyond agriculture. In any design context:

| Domain | Earth Care | People Care | Fair Share |
|--------|-----------|-------------|------------|
| **Ecological** | Soil, water, biodiversity | Food, shelter, health | Surplus redistributed to commons |
| **Organisational** | Institutional health; don't exhaust the system | Participant wellbeing; needs met | Limit resource hoarding; share knowledge |
| **Creative practice** | Studio ecology; sustain the conditions for making | The maker's health and agency | Share the work; don't extract from collaborators |
| **Digital** | Platform health; don't deplete the commons | User needs served, not exploited | Open source; limit rent-seeking |

## §3. The Twelve Principles (Holmgren)

Holmgren's (2002) articulation of twelve design principles is the system's operational core. Each principle operates simultaneously as ecological instruction and ethical injunction — a dual reading that is one of permaculture's distinctive contributions.

Krebs & Bach (2018) systematically validated all twelve principles against peer-reviewed scientific literature, confirming that each corresponds to documented ecological mechanisms.

### The Principles

| # | Principle | Ecological Reading | General Design Reading |
|---|-----------|-------------------|----------------------|
| 1 | **Observe and interact** | Read the site before designing; understand existing patterns, flows, edges | Understand the system before intervening; protracted observation before action |
| 2 | **Catch and store energy** | Harvest water, sunlight, biomass at peak availability for use in lean periods | Capture resources (knowledge, attention, capital) when abundant; design for storage |
| 3 | **Obtain a yield** | Every design must produce something useful; no purely theoretical systems | Ensure the system delivers value to its participants; yield justifies investment |
| 4 | **Apply self-regulation and accept feedback** | Design systems that self-correct; observe what the system is telling you | Build in feedback loops; accept correction from the system's own behaviour |
| 5 | **Use and value renewable resources and services** | Prefer biological over fossil; let living systems do the work | Prefer sustainable inputs; draw on self-replenishing resources |
| 6 | **Produce no waste** | Every output is an input for another element; closed-loop cycling | Design so that by-products are resources; eliminate structural waste |
| 7 | **Design from patterns to details** | Read large-scale patterns (watershed, wind, slope) before placing individual elements | Understand the macro before optimising the micro; patterns first, components second |
| 8 | **Integrate rather than segregate** | Place elements in relationship; the connections between elements are as important as the elements | Design for synergy; the value is in the relationships, not the parts |
| 9 | **Use small and slow solutions** | Small systems are more observable, manageable, and responsive to feedback | Start small; iterate; prefer slow accumulation over rapid scaling |
| 10 | **Use and value diversity** | Polyculture is more resilient than monoculture; diversity buffers against shock | Diverse systems are more robust; avoid single points of failure |
| 11 | **Use edges and value the marginal** | Interfaces between systems (forest/meadow, land/water) are the most productive zones | The most creative work happens at the boundaries between disciplines, perspectives, or practices |
| 12 | **Creatively use and respond to change** | Work with succession, not against it; design for temporal evolution | Treat change as material, not obstacle; design with time, not against it |

### Structural Observation

The principles form three clusters:

- **Principles 1–3:** Orientation — *how to see* (observe, catch, yield)
- **Principles 4–6:** Regulation — *how to govern* (self-regulate, renew, cycle)
- **Principles 7–12:** Design — *how to arrange* (patterns, integration, scale, diversity, edge, change)

This progression from perception to governance to arrangement mirrors the design process itself: see first, regulate second, compose third.

## §4. The Spatial Grammar: Zones and Sectors

### Zone Planning

The zone model is permaculture's most transposable construct — a spatial grammar for organising attention, energy, and interaction frequency.

```
         Zone 5 — Wilderness / unmanaged / observation only
       Zone 4 — Semi-wild / long rotation / forestry
     Zone 3 — Farm / orchard / infrequent management
   Zone 2 — Intensive cultivation / daily-to-weekly
 Zone 0 — Home / self / centre of activity
```

Management intensity radiates outward from Zone 0. The logic is simple: **place elements that need frequent attention close; place elements that need infrequent attention far.** This minimises wasted energy (fewer unnecessary trips to distant elements) and maximises responsive care (what needs daily tending is within arm's reach).

### Transposability of Zones

The zone model generalises to any system where proximity correlates with interaction frequency:

| Zone | Ecological | Studio Practice | Knowledge Work | Organisational |
|------|-----------|----------------|----------------|----------------|
| **0** | Home / body | The desk; the daily tool | Active working memory; today's document | Core team; daily standup |
| **1** | Kitchen garden; daily harvest | Materials in reach; sketchbook | Weekly review; active projects | Direct reports; weekly 1:1 |
| **2** | Orchard; weekly tending | Stored canvases; reference library | Monthly planning; archived projects | Department; monthly review |
| **3** | Pasture; seasonal rotation | Storage; seasonal media rotation | Quarterly strategy; long-horizon reading | Division; quarterly alignment |
| **4** | Managed forest; annual check | Deep archive; old portfolios | Annual reflection; dormant research | Organisation-wide; annual retreat |
| **5** | Wilderness; observation only | What you don't touch; what self-organises | The unread shelf; serendipitous encounter | The market; the ecosystem; what you watch but don't control |

**The design insight:** Misplacing elements across zones — putting a daily-need item in Zone 4, or putting a rarely-used resource in Zone 0 — generates friction proportional to the mismatch. Zone diagnosis asks: *is this element in the right zone for how often I actually interact with it?*

### Sector Analysis

Sectors map external energies flowing through a system: sun, wind, water, fire risk, noise, pollution, views. Sectors are forces you manage *around*, not *against*.

**General design reading:** What external forces flow through your system that you cannot control but must design for? Market conditions, policy changes, seasonal rhythms, upstream dependencies — these are sectors. The designer orients elements to catch beneficial flows and buffer harmful ones.

## §5. Design Constructs

### Stacking Functions

> *"Each element performs many functions; each important function is supported by multiple elements."*
> — Mollison (1988)

A single element (a tree, a tool, a protocol) should serve multiple purposes. A single important function (water supply, income, safety) should be supported by multiple elements. This produces redundancy through diversity — the system's resilience signature.

**General design reading:** In any system, ask two questions:
1. **For each element:** What else could this serve? (Stack functions on elements.)
2. **For each critical function:** What else supports this? (Stack elements on functions.)

A protocol that only serves one purpose is under-designed. A critical function supported by only one element is brittle.

### Guilds

A guild is an assemblage of elements that mutually benefit one another — the polyculture unit. The classic example: the "Three Sisters" (maize, beans, squash) where maize provides structure, beans fix nitrogen, and squash shades soil.

**General design reading:** Which elements in your system have complementary needs and outputs? Design clusters of mutual benefit, not collections of independent components.

### Edge Effect

> *"The interface between two ecosystems is more productive than either alone."*

Boundaries between systems — forest/meadow, land/water, disciplines/practices — are the most diverse and productive zones. Design to maximise productive edge.

**General design reading:** The most generative work happens at the interface between domains. Interdisciplinary boundaries, cross-team collaborations, the seam between structure and emergence — these are edges. Permaculture says: don't minimise edges; maximise them and design them well.

### Succession

Natural systems evolve through stages: bare soil → pioneer species → shrubs → forest. Permaculture designs *with* this temporal process rather than freezing systems at a single stage.

**General design reading:** Systems mature. Design for the trajectory, not just the current state. What does this system want to become? Work with the natural direction of development rather than maintaining a fixed configuration by force.

### Yield

> *"The yield of a system is theoretically unlimited, or only limited by the imagination and information of the designer."*
> — Mollison (1988)

Yield is the sum of useful outputs. But permaculture's insight is that yield is not extracted — it **emerges from well-designed relationships**. A poorly designed system produces little regardless of inputs. A well-designed system produces more than the sum of its parts because the relationships between elements generate surplus.

## §6. The And-Yet (Shadow)

### 6.1 The Science Gap

Permaculture emerged from practitioner observation, not from peer-reviewed research. Ferguson & Lovell (2014) documented the movement's isolation from mainstream agricultural science — claims often exceeded evidence, and the practitioner community was slow to engage with academic institutions. This gap is closing: Forte Taylor et al. (2025) document rapid growth in peer-reviewed publications from 2011 onward, and Schmid et al. (2024) provide rigorous empirical evidence in the *Nature* portfolio. But the historical pattern means that many permaculture claims in popular literature remain unvalidated. The lib entry should be read with this caveat: the *principles* have been scientifically validated (Krebs & Bach 2018); many *specific claims* in the practitioner literature have not.

### 6.2 The Scalability Question

Fiebrig et al. (2020) found significant gaps between permaculture aspirations and commercial farming realities. The design system works demonstrably well at small scales (home garden through small farm); its application to large-scale commercial agriculture remains unproven. This matters for intellectual honesty, though it matters less for the repository's use case (permaculture as design vocabulary rather than farming prescription).

### 6.3 Indigenous Knowledge Attribution

Mollison drew extensively on Indigenous Australian land management practices, and on traditional agricultural systems worldwide (Fukuoka, the Three Sisters, keyline water management from Yeomans' observation of Aboriginal land use). Attribution was incomplete — particularly in early works. The permaculture movement has acknowledged this unevenly. Some practitioners have engaged seriously with Indigenous knowledge holders; others have perpetuated extractive patterns of appropriation — using Indigenous techniques without adequate recognition or reciprocity.

This is not peripheral. For a design system grounded in the ethic of Fair Share, the failure to adequately share credit with knowledge sources is a violation of its own axiological foundation. The And-Yet here is structural, not incidental.

### 6.4 Practitioner Overreach

The permaculture movement includes a wide spectrum of rigour. At one end: careful, observation-based design supported by emerging empirical evidence. At the other: inflated claims about reversing desertification, solving food crises, or healing ecosystems through weekend workshops. The lib entry works from the validated principles (Holmgren 2002, Krebs & Bach 2018) rather than the aspirational rhetoric. Users should distinguish between the design methodology and the movement's promotional culture.

### 6.5 The Certification Shadow

The 72-hour Permaculture Design Certificate (PDC) has no standardised curriculum, no accreditation body, and no quality control mechanism. This means "permaculture designer" is a self-assigned title backed by wildly varying levels of training. The design system itself is robust; the credentialing ecosystem around it is not. The parallel to "regenerative" as a marketing term (see REGENERATIVE_CULTURES §7.3) is uncomfortable and direct.

### 6.6 The Self-Referential Test

> *If the twelve principles were applied to the permaculture movement itself, where would it fail?*

- **Principle 4 (Accept feedback):** The movement has historically been slow to accept feedback from scientific critique.
- **Principle 9 (Small and slow):** The global spread of PDC curricula was rapid, not slow; standardised, not place-adapted.
- **Principle 11 (Value the marginal):** Indigenous knowledge holders — the actual marginal voices — have been under-credited.

A design system that advocates observation, feedback, and humility must practise these values internally. Where it does not, the failure is diagnostic — the same tools that identify external problems illuminate internal ones.

## Integration with [CARDS](CARDS.md)

Permaculture's ethics triad maps to CARDS with remarkable directness. The mapping is not analogical but structural: both systems ground design in human needs, and both treat axiological failure as diagnostic.

### Forward Mapping: Constructs → CARDS Axes Served

| Construct | Primary CARDS | Expression |
|-----------|:-------------:|------------|
| **Earth Care** | **S** | Ecological safety as the container for all other needs |
| **People Care** | **R, D** | Relatedness (connection, belonging) and Dignity (needs met without exploitation) |
| **Fair Share** | **A** | Limits to accumulation enable others' autonomy; surplus redistributed |
| **Observe and interact** | **C** | Competence built through protracted observation before action |
| **Obtain a yield** | **C, A** | Competence (the system works) and Autonomy (participants receive value) |
| **Use edges and value the marginal** | **D, R** | Dignity (marginal voices valued) and Relatedness (boundaries as sites of connection) |
| **Creatively use and respond to change** | **C, S** | Competence (adaptive capacity) and Safety (resilience under perturbation) |

### Reverse Mapping: CARDS Degradation → Permaculture Principle Violated

| CARD in Red | Indicates |
|:-----------:|-----------|
| **C** 🔴 | Observation skipped; feedback loops broken; designer imposed rather than read |
| **A** 🔴 | Fair Share violated; accumulation unchecked; participants cannot access resources |
| **R** 🔴 | Integration neglected; elements segregated; community design absent |
| **D** 🔴 | People Care reduced to instrumentality; marginal voices excluded; Indigenous knowledge unattributed |
| **S** 🔴 | Earth Care abandoned; ecological foundation degraded; system cannot sustain itself |

### Diagnostic Protocol

1. **Observe** CARDS degradation (any axis showing 🔴 or 🟡).
2. **Ask:** Which ethic has been violated? (Earth Care → S; People Care → R, D; Fair Share → A)
3. **Identify** which principle has been neglected. (No observation? No feedback? No diversity? No edge?)
4. **Trace** the design failure: Is an element in the wrong zone? Is a critical function unsupported? Is an edge being minimised rather than cultivated?
5. **Redesign** using the relevant principle as corrective.
6. **Verify** CARDS recovery.

## Integration with [Regenerative Cultures](REGENERATIVE_CULTURES.md)

The connection is **genealogical and operational**. Permaculture is a primary design methodology for creating regenerative cultures. Wahl cites permaculture; permaculture predates and partly enables Wahl's synthesis.

### The Relationship

| Regenerative Cultures | Permaculture |
|----------------------|-------------|
| Asks the orienting question: "How do we design cultures that are regenerative?" | Provides the design methodology: observation, ethics, principles, zones, guilds, succession |
| Articulates the spectrum (degenerative → regenerative) | Operationalises movement along the spectrum through place-adapted design |
| Scale-linking design (personal → planetary) | Zone planning as a spatial instantiation of scale-linking |
| Biocultural uniqueness of place | Every design begins with site observation; no universal prescriptions |
| The designer as facilitator of conditions | The permaculture designer reads the landscape, arranges elements, and lets the system self-organise |

**Strain point:** Regenerative Cultures operates at a more abstract, philosophical level (questions-based methodology, living-systems thinking). Permaculture is more concrete and operational (place this element here, in this zone, with these connections). The abstraction gap can cause confusion — "regenerative" can float free of practice, while "permaculture" can sink into technique without adequate reflection. The two need each other: permaculture without regenerative thinking becomes recipe-following; regenerative thinking without permaculture (or equivalent methodology) becomes aspiration without design.

## Integration with [Salutogenesis](SALUTOGENESIS.md)

Stoltz & Schaffer (2018) provide the empirical bridge: permaculture-style edible forest gardens create salutogenic affordances — environmental features that promote health by supporting Antonovsky's three SOC components.

### The Bridge

| SOC Component | Permaculture Expression | Evidence |
|---------------|------------------------|----------|
| **Comprehensibility** | Systems designed to be legible: zone logic, visible patterns, readable landscapes | Stoltz & Schaffer (2018): participants reported understanding ecological processes through hands-on engagement |
| **Manageability** | Yield as accessible resource; design that puts needed elements within reach (zone placement) | Stoltz & Schaffer (2018): participants reported agency and capacity through productive gardening |
| **Meaningfulness** | Ethical foundation (Earth Care, People Care, Fair Share) provides purpose; participation is inherently meaningful | Stoltz & Schaffer (2018): participants reported social cohesion, connection to place, and sense of contribution |

### Formal Mapping

```
Permaculture design
    └── Creates environments with:
            ├── Legible patterns → SOC: Comprehensibility
            ├── Accessible yields → SOC: Manageability
            └── Ethical purpose  → SOC: Meaningfulness
                    │
                    ▼
            Salutogenic affordances
                    │
                    ▼
            Health-creation (movement toward health-ease)
```

Permaculture does not merely *align with* salutogenesis — it **creates the material conditions** for SOC to develop. A well-designed permaculture system is a salutogenic environment by construction.

## Integration with [Infinite Games](INFINITE_GAMES.md)

### Garden as Shared Metaphor

Carse's Machine vs. Garden construct is permaculture's organising metaphor made philosophical. The garden — never complete, always growing, evaluated by capacity for ongoing life — is exactly what the permaculture designer cultivates. The machine — complete at construction, evaluated by performance against specification — is what the permaculture designer resists imposing.

### Structural Mapping

| Infinite Games | Permaculture | Shared Logic |
|---------------|-------------|--------------|
| Garden (never complete) | Succession (design with temporal evolution) | Living systems are ongoing processes, not finished products |
| Surprise as invitation | Observe and interact; creatively respond to change | The unexpected is information, not failure |
| Rules change during play | Principles adapted to place; no universal prescriptions | Context determines application |
| Voluntary participation | People Care; Fair Share; community design | Participants are free agents, not inputs |
| Poietai (joyful poets) | The designer as facilitator of conditions | Design originates what it cannot finish |

### The Operationalisation Claim

Permaculture operationalises what Carse poeticises. The twelve principles are a *design grammar* for infinite play in ecological and social systems. Zone planning is a spatial structure for infinite-game cultivation. Succession thinking is temporal infinite play. The ethics triad is a commitment to continuing the game.

**Strain point:** Carse's framework is deliberately non-operational ("tells you what to see, not what to do"). Permaculture is deliberately operational (72-hour PDC, specific techniques, site plans). The mapping holds at the level of orientation but diverges at the level of prescription — Carse would resist the idea that infinite play can be taught in a standardised curriculum.

## Integration with [Machines and Gardens](../../ideacubator/idea__machines_and_gardens.md) (Ideacubator)

Permaculture is the **concrete design methodology** for the machine-garden operational mode. The twelve principles are the machine (structure, scaffold, protocol); the yields, succession, and self-organisation are the garden (emergence, growth, ongoing life). The ethics triad is the commitment that the machine serves the garden, never the reverse.

The machine-garden idea asks: "What happens at the seam where structure enables emergence?" Permaculture answers: **design happens.** Specifically: observe the existing patterns, arrange elements so their relationships generate yields, and then step back. The designer builds the machine; the garden grows itself.

**Failure mode correspondence:** Mechanisation (MG-1) in Carse's terms = imposing a blueprint on a living system in permaculture's terms. The reciprocal failure (the machine-garden idea's open question: "What if a machine is treated as a garden?") = permaculture without principles, randomness without design, Zone 5 everywhere with no Zone 0. The twelve principles *are* the machine that prevents the garden from dissolving into chaos.

## Execution Routines

### Routine A: Zone Diagnostic (Assessing a System)

Use when: evaluating whether elements in any system are well-placed relative to interaction frequency.

1. **Name Zone 0.** What is the centre of activity? (The desk, the daily standup, the body, the home.)
2. **List the elements.** What are the key components of this system?
3. **For each element, ask:** How frequently do I actually interact with this? Daily? Weekly? Monthly? Annually? Never?
4. **Map to zones.** Daily = Zone 0–1. Weekly = Zone 2. Monthly = Zone 3. Seasonal = Zone 4. Never/observation-only = Zone 5.
5. **Identify mismatches.** Is a daily-use element in Zone 4? Is a rarely-used element cluttering Zone 0?
6. **Redesign.** Move elements to their appropriate zone. The principle is: **proximity should match interaction frequency.**
7. **Verify with CARDS.** Does the new arrangement serve Competence (things are findable and usable), Autonomy (I can access what I need), and Safety (the system is navigable)?

### Routine B: Stacking Audit (Strengthening a Design)

Use when: designing or reviewing a system for resilience and efficiency.

1. **List elements.** What components exist in this system?
2. **For each element, ask:** What functions does it currently serve? What *additional* functions could it serve? (Stack functions on elements.)
3. **List critical functions.** What must this system reliably do?
4. **For each critical function, ask:** How many elements support this function? If only one, the function is **brittle**. (Stack elements on functions.)
5. **Identify brittleness.** Any critical function supported by a single element is a vulnerability.
6. **Redesign for redundancy.** Add supporting elements for brittle functions; add functions to under-utilised elements.
7. **Check for waste.** Is any output going unused? Can it become an input for another element?

### Routine C: Edge Scan (Finding Generative Boundaries)

Use when: seeking creative or productive opportunities in a system.

1. **Identify boundaries.** Where do different domains, disciplines, teams, or practices meet?
2. **Assess each edge.** Is this boundary currently: (a) minimised (the two domains don't interact), (b) contested (conflict at the boundary), or (c) productive (generative exchange)?
3. **For unproductive edges:** What elements could be placed *at* the boundary to create exchange? (A shared project, a cross-functional role, a meeting point.)
4. **For productive edges:** What makes this edge work? Can the pattern be replicated at other boundaries?
5. **Apply Principle 11:** *Use edges and value the marginal.* The most innovative work in the system is likely happening at its edges. Are these edges visible, supported, and valued?

## Why This Matters for the Repository

1. **Design methodology bridge:** Permaculture fills the gap between the repository's axiological frameworks (CARDS, Salutogenesis) and its design vocabularies (Parametric Authorship, Wardley Maps). It is a *design methodology grounded in ethics* — the operational grammar for translating values into practice.

2. **Spatial vocabulary:** Zone planning and sector analysis extend the repository's spatial thinking — already present in SRII's spatio-relational framing — with a concrete *design grammar* of proximity, attention, and interaction frequency. SRII establishes that intelligence is always situated; permaculture provides the operational method for organising that situation.

3. **Temporal vocabulary:** Succession thinking introduces a temporal dimension — designing with natural development trajectories rather than fixing systems at a single state. This complements Wardley Maps' evolution axis with an ecological perspective.

4. **Salutogenic affordance creation:** Stoltz & Schaffer (2018) demonstrate empirically that permaculture design creates the material conditions for health-creation (SOC components). This bridges SALUTOGENESIS and REGENERATIVE_CULTURES through measurable practice.

5. **Machine-garden instantiation:** The twelve principles (machine) enabling ecological and social emergence (garden) is the concrete design methodology for the machine-garden operational mode described in the ideacubator.

6. **Transposable design constructs:** Zones, stacking functions, edge effects, and succession are substrate-independent — they apply to studio practice, organisational design, knowledge management, and digital systems with the same structural logic as to ecological systems.

7. **Parametric authorship alignment:** The permaculture designer as parametric author — setting constraints (zone boundaries, guild compositions, edge ratios) within which the system generates its own yields. Design ≠ determination; design = conditions for emergence.
