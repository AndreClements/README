## Attribution & Ownership

**The Drama Triangle is a social model of human interaction developed by Stephen B. Karpman, M.D., in 1968.**

Karpman developed the model while studying under Eric Berne, founder of Transactional Analysis (TA). The framework maps three habitual psychological roles that people adopt in dysfunctional social dynamics: **Persecutor**, **Victim**, and **Rescuer**. The model earned Karpman the Eric Berne Memorial Scientific Award in 1972.

**Key Works:**
- Karpman, S.B. 1968. Fairy tales and script drama analysis. *Transactional Analysis Bulletin*, 7(26):39-43.
- Karpman, S.B. 2014. *A game free life: the definitive book on the Drama Triangle and Compassion Triangle*. San Francisco: Drama Triangle Publications.
- Berne, E. 1964. *Games people play: the psychology of human relationships*. New York: Grove Press.

**Lineage:**
```
Psychoanalysis (Freud)

## §1. The Three Roles

### 1.1 Victim

> *"Poor me!"*

The Victim position is characterised by helplessness, hopelessness, and powerlessness. The Victim feels oppressed, ashamed, and unable to make decisions or solve problems.

**Core belief:** "I cannot do anything; nothing can be done."

**Dynamics:**
- Seeks Rescuers to confirm helplessness
- Invites Persecutors to validate victimhood
- Unconsciously maintains the drama by refusing agency

**Note:** This is a *role*, not an identity. A person genuinely victimised can respond from agency; a person in the Victim *role* responds from learned helplessness regardless of actual circumstances.

### 1.2 Persecutor

> *"It's all your fault!"*

The Persecutor position is characterised by blame, criticism, control, and superiority. The Persecutor maintains power through judgement and intimidation.

**Core belief:** "I am right; you are wrong."

**Dynamics:**
- Sets strict rules and punishes violations
- May have been victimised and now displaces onto others (see destructive entitlement in [MULTI_PARTIALITY](MULTI_PARTIALITY.md))
- Switches to Victim when confronted ("Now *I'm* being attacked!")

### 1.3 Rescuer

> *"Let me help you."*

The Rescuer position is characterised by compulsive helping, guilt-driven intervention, and enabling of dependency. The Rescuer needs someone to save.

**Core belief:** "You can't do it without me."

**Dynamics:**
- Helps even when help is unwanted or counterproductive
- Prevents Victims from experiencing natural consequences
- Creates dependency rather than capacity
- Switches to Persecutor when help fails ("After all I did for you!")
- Switches to Victim when unappreciated ("Nobody notices my sacrifice!")

## §2. Role Dynamics (Script Mechanics)

### 2.1 The Switch

The Drama Triangle is self-perpetuating through rapid, often unconscious **role switches**. A complete cycle might occur in seconds:

```
Rescuer: "Let me help you with that."
Victim:  "You're not doing it right!"  [→ switches to Persecutor]
Rescuer: "I was only trying to help!"  [→ switches to Victim]
Victim:  "I'm sorry, I shouldn't have snapped." [→ switches to Rescuer]
```

### 2.2 Script Velocity

Karpman introduced **Script Velocity** — the rate of role switches per unit time. Higher velocity means more emotional intensity and stronger entrenchment in the drama.

```
velocity = switches / time
drama_intensity ∝ velocity
```

### 2.3 Script Range

**Script Range** measures the emotional contrast between switched positions. A shift from dominating Persecutor to helpless Victim produces high range and dramatic intensity.

### 2.4 Why People Stay

The triangle persists because each role provides an unconscious payoff:

| Role | Payoff |
|------|--------|
| Victim | Avoids responsibility; receives attention; confirms worldview |
| Persecutor | Maintains control; feels superior; justifies anger |
| Rescuer | Feels needed; avoids own problems; justifies self-worth through others |

## §3. Exit Strategies (Alternative Triangles)

### 3.1 The Winner's Triangle (Choy 1990)

Acey Choy developed the Winner's Triangle as a game-free alternative:

| Drama Role | Winner's Role | Shift |
|------------|---------------|-------|
| Victim | **Vulnerable** | Acknowledge needs and feelings *with responsibility* |
| Persecutor | **Assertive** | Communicate boundaries *without blame* |
| Rescuer | **Caring** | Offer support *without taking over* |

**Key insight:** The Winner's Triangle doesn't eliminate the positions — it transforms their expression from discounting to respecting.

### 3.2 The Empowerment Dynamic (Emerald 2009)

David Emerald developed TED (The Empowerment Dynamic) as a positive reframe:

| Drama Role | TED Role | Orientation |
|------------|----------|-------------|
| Victim | **Creator** | Focus on desired outcomes, not problems |
| Persecutor | **Challenger** | Build others up, encourage growth |
| Rescuer | **Coach** | Support without enabling; ask, don't tell |

**Key insight:** TED shifts from reactive (responding to drama) to generative (creating desired states).

## §4. The And-Yet (Shadow)

The Drama Triangle, over-applied or misapplied, can harm:

### 4.1 Over-application Risks

- **Minimising real victimisation:** Not everyone in a Victim role is "playing victim." Genuine harm exists. The Drama Triangle describes a *relational pattern*, not an assessment of objective circumstances.
- **Weaponised labelling:** Calling someone "Persecutor" or "Victim" can become its own form of persecution.
- **Rescuer-shaming:** Some helping is legitimate. The model distinguishes *compulsive* rescuing from genuine care, but this nuance is often lost.

### 4.2 Known Limitations

- **Western/individualist frame:** The model assumes individual agency may be exercised. In collectivist or high-power-distance cultures, role dynamics operate differently.
- **Insufficient systemic analysis:** Structural power imbalances (race, gender, class) are not adequately addressed. Someone in a structurally oppressed position is not "playing Victim."
- **Binary simplification:** Three roles may not capture the complexity of all dysfunctional dynamics.

### 4.3 Scientific Status

- **Historical:** Limited empirical validation until recently
- **Current (2020+):** First validated measurement scale published (Lac & Donaldson 2020)
- **Evidence type:** Strong clinical consensus; growing quantitative research

### 4.4 External Critiques

Lac & Donaldson (2020:1) note: "Despite widespread use of the Drama Triangle in clinical and organisational settings, empirical research validating the construct has been limited."

### 4.5 The Meta-Question

> *When does the Drama Triangle itself become a Persecutor move?*

When used to dismiss legitimate grievances, the framework becomes a tool of the very dynamic it describes. Calling someone's pain a "Victim role" can be the ultimate Persecutor stance.

**Mitigation:** Use the triangle as a *pattern descriptor*, not a *character judgement*. The question is never "Are you a Victim?" but "Are we caught in a drama pattern?"

# Repository Integration

## What This Changes

The Drama Triangle inverts the conventional view of interpersonal conflict:

| Conventional View | Drama Triangle View |
|-------------------|---------------------|
| Conflict has perpetrators and victims | All three roles perpetuate the drama |
| The helper is neutral and benign | The Rescuer enables dysfunction |
| Roles are fixed by character | Roles switch rapidly within interactions |
| Resolution = assigning blame | Resolution = exiting the triangle entirely |

> *"The triangle is not a map of personality — it's a map of a game in progress."*

```

## Integration with Multi-Partiality

The Drama Triangle reveals **failed multi-partiality**:

| Multi-Partiality | Drama Collapse |
|------------------|----------------|
| Hold all perspectives | Collapse into single role |
| Acknowledge all entitlements | Discount others' legitimate claims |
| Credit contributions | Extract without acknowledgement |
| Seek rejunction | Perpetuate cycle |

**Insight:** Multi-partiality is the antidote to drama. When the host (therapist, facilitator, leader) holds all three positions simultaneously — acknowledging the Victim's pain, the Persecutor's frustration, and the Rescuer's intent — the triangle can dissolve.

**Protocol mapping:**
- Multi-partiality Routine B ("Credit before challenging") directly counters Persecutor stance
- Multi-partiality Routine B ("Represent the absent") prevents Victim erasure
- Multi-partiality Routine B ("Seek rejunction") offers exit from the game

## Execution Routines

### Routine A: Diagnostic (Am I in the Triangle?)

1. **Notice the feeling.** Righteous anger? Helplessness? Compulsive helpfulness?
2. **Check the payoff.** What am I getting from this position?
3. **Map the triangle.** Who's playing which role? Have we switched?
4. **Assess velocity.** How fast are we switching? (High velocity = deep entrenchment)

### Routine B: Exit (Self)

1. **Pause.** Break the automatic response cycle.
2. **Own your position.** "I notice I'm playing Rescuer right now."
3. **Shift to Winner's/TED role:**
   - Victim → Vulnerable: "I'm struggling, and I can still act."
   - Persecutor → Assertive: "I have a boundary, and I can state it without blame."
   - Rescuer → Caring: "I want to help, and I'll check if that's wanted."
4. **Check CARDS.** Is Dignity, Autonomy, Relatedness preserved for all?

### Routine C: Facilitation (Helping Others Exit)

1. **Map the triangle aloud.** Name the pattern without blame.
2. **Multi-partiality.** Acknowledge each position's legitimate concern.
3. **Offer the exit.** Introduce Winner's Triangle language.
4. **Model the shift.** Demonstrate Vulnerable/Assertive/Caring from your own position.
5. **Rejunction.** "What would balance look like here?"
