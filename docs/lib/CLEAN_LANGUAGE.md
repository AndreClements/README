## Attribution & Ownership

**Clean Language is an inquiry methodology developed by David Grove (1950-2008), a New Zealand counselling psychologist.**

Grove developed Clean Language in the 1980s while working with trauma survivors. He observed that clients naturally expressed their experiences through metaphor, and that conventional therapeutic questioning often contaminated these metaphors with the therapist's assumptions. Clean Language uses precisely structured questions with minimal presuppositions to explore clients' inner symbolic worlds without re-traumatisation.

The methodology was later formalised as **Symbolic Modelling** by James Lawley and Penny Tompkins (2000), who studied Grove's work extensively and integrated it with cognitive linguistics and self-organising systems theory.

**Key Works:**
- Grove, D.J. & Panzer, B.I. 1989. *Resolving traumatic memories: metaphors and symbols in psychotherapy*. New York: Irvington Publishers.
- Lawley, J. & Tompkins, P. 2000. *Metaphors in mind: transformation through symbolic modelling*. London: Developing Company Press.
- Sullivan, W. & Rees, J. 2008. *Clean language: revealing metaphors and opening minds*. Carmarthen: Crown House Publishing.
- Walker, C. 2014. *From contempt to curiosity: creating the conditions for groups to collaborate using Clean Language and Systemic Modelling*. Hampshire: Clean Publishing.

## §1. The Core Constructs

### 1.1 The "Clean" Principle

**"Clean"** means: minimal therapist projection.

Grove observed that many clients described their symptoms using metaphors drawn from previous therapists' language, not their own experience. Therapist influence had contaminated the client's symbolic world.

Clean Language addresses this by:
- Using the client's **exact words** (not paraphrases)
- Asking questions with **minimal presuppositions**
- Honouring **auditory components** (pace, pitch, tonality)
- Avoiding interpretation, reframe, or suggestion

**The Clean stance:** *"I do not know what this means for you. I will ask questions that help you discover what it means."*

### 1.2 The Clean Questions

Clean Language consists of a structured set of questions. The core questions fall into three categories:

#### Developing Questions (exploring what exists)

| Question | Purpose |
|----------|---------|
| "And is there anything else about [X]?" | Expands details |
| "And what kind of [X] is that [X]?" | Identifies attributes |
| "And where is [X]?" | Locates in space |
| "And whereabouts [X]?" | Specifies location |
| "And that's [X] like what?" | Elicits metaphor |
| "And is there a relationship between [X] and [Y]?" | Explores connections |
| "And when [X], what happens to [Y]?" | Explores effects |

#### Sequence Questions (exploring time)

| Question | Purpose |
|----------|---------|
| "And then what happens?" | Moves forward |
| "And what happens next?" | Continues sequence |
| "And what happens just before [X]?" | Moves backward |

#### Intention Questions (exploring agency)

| Question | Purpose |
|----------|---------|
| "And what would you like to have happen?" | Surfaces desired outcome |
| "And what needs to happen for [X] to [occur]?" | Identifies conditions |
| "And can [X] [happen]?" | Tests possibility |

#### Syntax Pattern

Clean questions typically begin with **"And"** — this:
- Acknowledges what the client said
- Directs attention to the next inquiry
- Signals continuity rather than interruption

### 1.3 Metaphor Landscapes

When Clean questions are asked iteratively, clients develop **metaphor landscapes** — interconnected symbolic structures that represent their inner experience.

```
Example development:
Q: "What would you like to have happen?"
A: "I'd like to feel more grounded."

Q: "And grounded. And when grounded, that's grounded like what?"
A: "Like a tree with deep roots."

Q: "And a tree with deep roots. And what kind of tree is that tree?"
A: "An old oak. Strong."

Q: "And where is that old oak?"
A: "It's... it's in my chest. Where my heart is."

→ Metaphor landscape: Old oak with deep roots, located in chest/heart
```

The landscape is not interpreted by the facilitator. It is **developed** by the client through Clean inquiry until transformation emerges.

### 1.4 Symbolic Modelling

**Symbolic Modelling** is Lawley and Tompkins' (2000) formalisation of Grove's method into a complete therapeutic process:

```
Symbolic Modelling Process:
1. Entry → Surface presenting condition and desired outcome
2. Developing → Build the metaphor landscape with Clean questions
3. Exploring → Identify relationships, binds, resources
4. Resolving → Facilitate landscape transformation (emerges, not imposed)
5. Maturing → Integrate changes; ground in everyday context
```

**Key principle:** The facilitator models the client's symbolic world in their own mind, then uses that model to select the next Clean question. The model remains implicit; the questions remain Clean.

## §2. Clean Space

Grove continued developing his work into the 2000s, evolving Clean Language into **Clean Space** — a spatial methodology where clients physically move around a room while exploring their symbolic world.

```
Clean Space syntax:
"Find a space that knows something about [X]"
"And what do you know from here?"
"And is there another space that knows something else?"
```

Clean Space adds embodiment and spatial cognition to the linguistic exploration of Clean Language.

## §3. The And-Yet (Shadow)

Clean Language, over-applied or misapplied, can harm:

### 3.1 Over-application Risks

- **Mechanical questioning:** Clean syntax without genuine curiosity becomes interrogation. The "And..." can feel relentless if not held with presence.
- **Avoidance of appropriate input:** Some situations require directive intervention (crisis, safety risk, information asymmetry). Clean is not always appropriate.
- **Fetishisation of metaphor:** Not all experience needs symbolic exploration. Sometimes practical problem-solving is more useful.

### 3.2 Known Limitations

- **Training required:** Effective Clean facilitation requires substantial practice. The questions look simple but their deployment requires skill.
- **Time-intensive:** Metaphor landscapes develop slowly. Not suited to time-pressured contexts.
- **Limited empirical validation:** While growing, peer-reviewed research remains sparse compared to mainstream therapies (Tosey, Lawley & Cairns-Lee 2021).

### 3.3 Scientific Status

- **Clinical consensus:** Strong acceptance in coaching and therapeutic communities
- **Peer-reviewed research:** Emerging (British Journal of Management 2014; recent healthcare studies)
- **Validated measurements:** Limited

### 3.4 Cultural Considerations

- **Language dependency:** Effectiveness may vary across languages with different metaphor structures
- **Western assumptions:** The individualist frame (explore *your* metaphor) may not suit collectivist contexts
- **Implicit assumptions:** Even "minimal presupposition" questions contain some cultural framing

### 3.5 The Meta-Question

> *When does "staying Clean" become its own form of withholding?*

If a client asks for information, guidance, or opinion, insisting on only asking Clean questions can become a game — the facilitator hiding behind technique. Clean is a tool, not a dogma.

**Mitigation:** Clean Language is appropriate for exploration. When the task is information transfer, direction, or decision, other modes are needed.

# Repository Integration

## What This Changes

Clean Language inverts the conventional inquiry stance:

| Conventional Inquiry | Clean Inquiry |
|----------------------|---------------|
| Therapist interprets client's experience | Client explores own symbolic world |
| Questions contain assumptions | Questions have minimal presuppositions |
| Metaphors are translated to "reality" | Metaphors are developed and explored as-is |
| Therapist's framework shapes understanding | Client's framework remains sovereign |
| Paraphrase demonstrates understanding | Exact words preserve meaning |

> *"Clean Language is deeply agreeable to the client's heart and soul."* — David Grove

## Integration with [CARDS](CARDS.md)

Clean Language inherently preserves all five [CARDS](CARDS.md) ([Je'anna L Clements](CARDS.md)):

### CARDS Preservation

| CARD | How Clean Language Preserves It |
|------|--------------------------------|
| **C** (Competence) | Client is the expert on their own experience; facilitator asks, not tells |
| **A** (Autonomy) | Client directs attention; no suggestions or interpretations imposed |
| **R** (Relatedness) | Genuine meeting through attentive presence; words honoured exactly |
| **D** (Dignity) | Client's symbolic world respected as meaningful; no reframe or correction |
| **S** (Safety) | No re-traumatisation; exploration at client's pace; metaphor provides distance |

### Diagnostic Use

```
If CARDS degradation detected in an interaction:
  Check facilitation for "unclean" patterns:
    C low → Am I telling rather than asking?
    A low → Am I directing where attention should go?
    R low → Am I paraphrasing instead of using exact words?
    D low → Am I interpreting or correcting metaphors?
    S low → Am I pushing toward content the client isn't ready for?

Intervention = return to Clean syntax
```

### CARDS-Clean Alignment

Clean Language can be understood as the **inquiry protocol that keeps all CARDS green**.

When we ask "What preserves Dignity in questioning?", Clean Language is the operational answer.

## Integration with Multi-Partiality

Clean Language provides the **verbal syntax** for multi-partial hosting:

### Alignment

| Multi-Partiality Principle | Clean Language Practice |
|----------------------------|------------------------|
| Acknowledge each perspective | Use client's exact words (each word honoured) |
| Don't collapse into one truth | Don't interpret (multiple meanings can coexist) |
| Credit contributions | Repeat back what was said (implicit crediting) |
| Represent the absent | "And is there anything else?" (invites what hasn't yet spoken) |

### Multi-Party Clean

In group facilitation, Clean syntax extends to holding multiple metaphor landscapes:

```
Person A: "I feel stuck, like I'm in mud."
Person B: "I feel pushed, like there's a hand on my back."

Facilitator (Clean, multi-partial):
  "And A, you're in mud. And B, there's a hand on your back.
   And is there a relationship between the mud and the hand?"

→ Neither metaphor is privileged; both are developed; relationship is explored
```

This is multi-partiality operationalised as inquiry syntax.

## Integration with METHODOLOGY_CII

Clean Language aligns with the [As-If / If-Not methodology](../methods/METHODOLOGY_CII.md):

### As-If Inquiry

Clean questions are inherently **As-If** — they assume provisionally that the client's metaphor is meaningful, then explore it:

```
As-If stance:
  "And that's [X] like what?"
  → Assumes X is like something; explores rather than judges

If-Not check:
  → If client says "It's not like anything", the question surfaces that too
```

### Clean as CI Protocol

```
CI Session using Clean:
  As-If pass: Ask Clean questions to develop the landscape
  If-Not pass: "And is there anything that doesn't fit?" / "And what's missing?"
  Validation: "And how do you know [transformation] has happened?"
```

## Execution Routines

### Routine A: Basic Clean Inquiry

**Use when:** Exploring someone's experience without contamination

1. **Listen for key words** — nouns, verbs, metaphors, unusual phrasing
2. **Reflect exact words back** — "And [X]..."
3. **Ask a developing question** — "And what kind of [X] is that [X]?"
4. **Follow the client's attention** — ask about what they emphasise
5. **Repeat** — develop the landscape iteratively

**Trap to avoid:** Paraphrasing. "So what you're saying is..." is not Clean.

### Routine B: Outcome Orientation

**Use when:** Starting a Clean session or re-orienting

1. **Ask:** "And what would you like to have happen?"
2. **Develop the desired outcome** — "And when [outcome], then what happens?"
3. **Explore conditions** — "And what needs to happen for [outcome] to happen?"
4. **Resource** — "And where could [resource] come from?"

### Routine C: Metaphor Development

**Use when:** Client offers a metaphor

1. **Receive the metaphor** — "And [metaphor]..."
2. **Locate it** — "And where is [metaphor]?"
3. **Characterise it** — "And what kind of [metaphor] is that?"
4. **Explore relationships** — "And is there anything else about [metaphor]?"
5. **Test movement** — "And what happens next?"
