## Attribution & Ownership

**Regenerative Cultures is a framework developed by Daniel Christian Wahl**, a German-born biologist, designer, and educator. Wahl holds a BSc in Biology (Edinburgh), an MSc in Holistic Science (Schumacher College / Plymouth), and a PhD in Natural Design (University of Dundee, supervised by Seaton Baxter). He is a Fellow of the Royal Society of Arts (FRSA), a recipient of the RSA Bicentenary Medal (2021), and has served as consultant to Gaia Education, the International Futures Forum, and Schumacher College.

First synthesised in his doctoral work *Design for Human and Planetary Health* (2006) and elaborated in *Designing Regenerative Cultures* (Triarchy Press, 2016; 2nd ed. 2022), the framework integrates salutogenesis, living-systems thinking, and ecological design into a questions-based methodology for cultural transformation.

## §1. The Utility (Why Import This?)

Regenerative Cultures inverts the dominant sustainability question:

| Sustainability Paradigm | Regenerative Paradigm |
|--------------------------|------------------------|
| "How do we do less damage?" | "How do we actively restore life?" |
| Harm reduction | Health creation |
| Net zero | Net positive |
| Universal standards | Place-adapted cultures |
| Prescriptive solutions | Questions-based inquiry |
| Single-scale intervention | Scale-linking design |

**Why this matters for the repository:**

1. **Scale-linking bridge:** Extends the existing [Salutogenesis](SALUTOGENESIS.md) framework (individual health-creation) to planetary health-creation — from SOC to salutogenic design to regenerative cultures. The genealogical chain is direct, not analogical.
2. **Salutogenic design:** Wahl & Baxter (2008) coined the term, creating a peer-reviewed articulation of how Antonovsky's principles translate into design practice across scales. This extends a lib entry we already host.
3. **Living-systems bridge:** The framework's dependence on autopoiesis, Santiago Theory, and self-organisation prepares conceptual ground for the planned Autopoiesis lib entry.
4. **Parametric alignment:** The questions-based methodology aligns with Parametric Authorship — the designer sets parameters (questions, principles); cultures self-author within constraints. The regenerative designer is not prescriber but facilitator.

## §2. The Core Question

> *"How do we design cultures that are regenerative rather than degenerative — cultures that heal, restore, and create conditions conducive to life?"*

Wahl observed that:
- The converging ecological, social, and economic crises are not separate problems but symptoms of a single root cause
- That root cause is a **crisis of perception** — the epistemological separation of nature from culture (via Bateson, Capra)
- Sustainability, understood as "doing less damage," is a necessary but insufficient response
- The adequate response is the active design of *regenerative* cultures: diverse, place-adapted, life-affirming ways of living

This reframes ecological design as **cultural design** — not technical problem-solving but the transformation of worldviews, values, and relationships that shape all downstream material choices.

> *"Sustainability is not enough — we need regenerative cultures."*
> — Daniel Christian Wahl

## §3. Regenerative vs. Sustainable

The central conceptual move is a spectrum, not a binary:

```
Degenerative ◄──────────────────────────────────────────────► Regenerative
    │              │              │              │              │
 Extractive    Conventional    Sustainable    Restorative    Regenerative
 (net loss)    (status quo)    (net zero)     (repair)       (net positive)
```

### Definitions

| Level | Orientation | Question |
|-------|-------------|----------|
| **Degenerative** | Extracting more than systems can renew | "How do we maximise short-term yield?" |
| **Conventional** | Business as usual; externalising costs | "How do we meet current demand?" |
| **Sustainable** | Reducing harm; efficiency metrics | "How do we do less damage?" |
| **Restorative** | Repairing past damage; remediation | "How do we fix what we broke?" |
| **Regenerative** | Creating conditions for life to thrive | "How do we actively improve systemic health?" |

Following Reed (2007), these levels are **nested**, not sequential — regenerative practice includes and transcends the lower levels. Sustainability metrics remain necessary; they are simply insufficient on their own.

**Key insight:** The shift from sustainable to regenerative is not incremental improvement but a change in *kind* — from managing damage to creating health. This parallels Antonovsky's inversion: pathogenesis asks what causes disease; salutogenesis asks what creates health.

## §4. Salutogenic Design

The concept of **salutogenic design** was coined by Wahl & Baxter (2008) in *Design Issues* (MIT Press), directly extending Antonovsky's salutogenesis into design practice.

### The Extension

| Salutogenesis (Antonovsky) | Salutogenic Design (Wahl & Baxter) |
|---------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Individual health-creation | Systemic health-creation across scales |
| Sense of Coherence (SOC) | Design for comprehensibility, manageability, meaningfulness at system level |
| Generalised Resistance Resources (GRRs) | Design conditions that generate collective GRRs |
| "How do people stay well?" | "How do we design systems that generate health?" |

### SOC at Design Scale

The three components of Sense of Coherence translate into design principles:

| SOC Component | Design Expression |
|---------------|-------------------|
| **Comprehensibility** | Systems that are legible — participants can understand the patterns and processes they are part of |
| **Manageability** | Systems that provide resources and agency — participants can act meaningfully within them |
| **Meaningfulness** | Systems that generate belonging and purpose — participation feels worth the investment |

```
Salutogenic_Design := Design_for(Comprehensibility, Manageability, Meaningfulness)
                      across_scales(individual → community → bioregion → planet)
```

### The Designer as Facilitator

Wahl reframes the designer's role: not as the author of solutions but as the **facilitator of conditions** from which healthy solutions emerge. The designer asks good questions, makes connections visible, and sets parameters — cultures self-organise within those parameters.

## §5. Scale-Linking Design

Design interventions must connect across scales. No single scale suffices.

```
Personal ──→ Community ──→ Bioregional ──→ National ──→ Planetary
    │              │              │              │              │
    └──────────────┴──────────────┴──────────────┴──────────────┘
                    Nested adaptive cycles (panarchy)
```

### The Principle

Drawing on Gunderson & Holling's **panarchy** model, Wahl argues that living systems operate as nested adaptive cycles at multiple scales. Actions at any single scale propagate upward and downward:

- A personal practice shift (diet, transport) aggregates into community patterns
- A community initiative (food cooperative, energy commons) shapes bioregional conditions
- A bioregional strategy (watershed management, land trust) constrains and enables national policy
- Planetary conditions (climate, biodiversity) loop back to constrain all scales

**No single-scale intervention suffices.** A sustainability strategy that operates only at the policy level (top-down) or only at the individual level (bottom-up) misses the coupling between scales. Regenerative design is explicitly **scale-linking** — connecting interventions vertically through nested systems.

### Formal Statement

```
For any intervention I at scale S_n:
  Health(I) := f(coherence_at(S_n), coupling_to(S_n-1), coupling_to(S_n+1))

An intervention that optimises for a single scale while degrading adjacent scales is degenerative,
even if locally sustainable.
```

## §6. Biocultural Uniqueness of Place

Regenerative cultures are necessarily **plural**. There is no universal blueprint for a regenerative culture — each must emerge from the specific ecological, social, cultural, and historical conditions of its place.

### The Principle

| Universal Approach | Place-Adapted Approach |
|--------------------|-----------------------|
| One solution for all contexts | Solutions emerge from local conditions |
| Expert-designed, applied top-down | Community-facilitated, grown from soil |
| Scalable uniformity | Diverse biocultural expressions |
| Efficiency metrics | Health-of-place metrics |

Wahl draws on the concept of **bioregionalism** — the recognition that human cultures are (or should be) adapted to the ecological realities of their bioregion. A regenerative culture in a coastal Mediterranean ecosystem will differ fundamentally from one in a semi-arid savanna or a boreal forest.

**Key insight:** This opposes the universalising tendency of both industrial capitalism and certain strands of sustainability science. The questions remain universal ("How do we create conditions conducive to life?"); the answers are necessarily local.

### Connection to Landscapification

The biocultural uniqueness of place connects directly to the repository's concept of Landscapification — environment as continuous territory rather than discrete objects; identity as emerging from place-based relationships rather than imposed categories.

## §7. Appropriate Participation

Rather than controlling nature, design should enable humans to **participate** in natural processes.

### Biomimicry vs. Bionics

Wahl (2006) distinguishes two orientations toward nature:

| Bionics | Biomimicry |
|---------|------------|
| Extract functional principles from nature to serve human purposes | Learn from nature to design systems that integrate with natural processes |
| Nature as resource to mine | Nature as mentor to learn from |
| Control orientation | Participation orientation |
| "How can we use this?" | "How can we fit in?" |

**Appropriate participation** means designing *with* living systems rather than *against* them. This requires understanding those systems as self-organising, self-creating entities — which connects directly to **autopoiesis** (Maturana & Varela, 1980) and the **Santiago Theory of Cognition**.

### Living-Systems Thinking

The framework's theoretical foundation rests on whole-systems / living-systems thinking:
- **Autopoiesis:** Living systems are self-creating; they produce and maintain themselves through their own operations
- **Santiago Theory:** Cognition is the process of life itself; living is knowing
- **Self-organisation:** Order emerges from the dynamics of the system, not from external command
- **Structural coupling:** Organisms and environments co-evolve through ongoing mutual perturbation

Regenerative design works *with* these processes rather than against them. The designer who understands autopoiesis does not design the culture; they design the conditions from which the culture can self-create.

## §8. Integration with CARDS

The CARDS framework ([Je'anna L Clements](CARDS.md)) maps to Regenerative Cultures:

### Forward Mapping: Constructs → CARDS Axes Served

| Construct | Primary CARDS | Expression |
|-----------|:-------------:|------------|
| **Regenerative vs. sustainable** | **S** | Net-positive health-creation actively improves Safety conditions |
| **Salutogenic design** | **C, R** | Builds Competence (systemic literacy) and Relatedness (community health-creation) |
| **Scale-linking design** | **R, S** | Connects individual Relatedness to collective Safety across scales |
| **Biocultural uniqueness** | **A, D** | Respects Autonomy (no universal prescription) and Dignity (cultural identity valued) |
| **Appropriate participation** | **R, D** | Extends Relatedness to non-human entities; Dignity to future generations |
| **Crisis of perception** | **C** | Diagnoses a Competence deficit — the inability to perceive interconnection |

### Reverse Mapping: CARDS Degradation → Regenerative Principle Violated

| CARD in Red | Indicates |
|:-----------:|-----------|
| **C** 🔴 | Crisis of perception unaddressed; systemic illiteracy; scale-blindness |
| **A** 🔴 | Universalising approach imposed; place-based autonomy overridden |
| **R** 🔴 | Nature/culture separation active; community relationships extractive |
| **D** 🔴 | Non-human entities or future generations treated as resources, not stakeholders |
| **S** 🔴 | Degenerative or merely sustainable practice; net-positive health-creation absent |

### Diagnostic Integration

Regenerative cultures are the conditions under which CARDS can be collectively met at scale:

```
Regenerative_Culture := system where:
  ∀ scale S_n:
    CARDS(participants_at(S_n)).trend → green
    AND coupling_to(S_n-1, S_n+1) is health-creating, not extractive
```

CARDS provides the individual-scale diagnostic that Wahl's framework lacks. Wahl provides the multi-scale design methodology that CARDS alone cannot offer. The integration is **complementary**, not redundant.

## §9. Integration with Salutogenesis

The connection between Regenerative Cultures and [Salutogenesis](SALUTOGENESIS.md) is **genealogical**, not analogical. Wahl directly extends Antonovsky.

### The Lineage

```
Antonovsky (1979)
    └── Sense of Coherence (SOC) — individual health-creation
            └── Wahl & Baxter (2008)
                    └── Salutogenic Design — systemic health-creation across scales
                            └── Wahl (2016)
                                    └── Regenerative Cultures — cultural health-creation
```

### GRRs at Collective Scale

Antonovsky's Generalised Resistance Resources (GRRs) operate at the individual level. Regenerative design extends them to collective scales:

| GRR Category | Individual (Antonovsky) | Collective (Wahl) |
|--------------|------------------------|-------------------|
| **Material** | Money, shelter, food | Commons, shared infrastructure, bioregional resources |
| **Knowledge** | Education, skills | Ecological literacy, traditional ecological knowledge |
| **Identity** | Ego identity | Biocultural identity; sense of place |
| **Social** | Relationships, community | Reciprocal human-nature-community relationships |
| **Cultural** | Shared meaning systems | Place-adapted cultural practices |

### Strain Point

Wahl extends Antonovsky's empirically grounded framework (SOC has been validated across 40+ countries) into a normative design methodology with less empirical validation. The salutogenic design concept is theoretically rigorous (peer-reviewed in *Design Issues*) but operationally less tested than SOC itself. The extension is intellectually honest — Wahl cites Antonovsky explicitly — but users should note that the further from the original SOC research, the more the evidence base thins.

## §10. Execution Routines

### Routine A: Regenerative Assessment (Diagnostic)

Assess where a system, project, or practice sits on the degenerative–regenerative spectrum:

1. **Map the spectrum.** Where does the current practice sit? Degenerative, conventional, sustainable, restorative, or regenerative?
2. **Check scale-linking.** Does the intervention connect across scales, or does it optimise for one scale while degrading others?
3. **Assess net direction.** Is the overall trajectory toward health-creation (regenerative) or harm-reduction-at-best (sustainable)?
4. **CARDS check.** Are CARDS trending green for participants at all affected scales?

### Routine B: Questions-Based Design (Generative)

Apply the questions-based methodology to a design challenge:

1. **Frame the question, not the answer.** What question, asked well, would open the space for regenerative solutions? (e.g., "How might this bioregion become more alive because of our presence?")
2. **Honour biocultural uniqueness.** What are the specific ecological, social, and cultural conditions of this place? What does *this* place need?
3. **Facilitate, don't prescribe.** Set parameters (constraints, values, questions) within which participants can self-organise. The designer tends soil conditions; the garden grows itself.
4. **Iterate through inquiry.** Return to the question. Has the answer changed? Has the question deepened?

### Routine C: Scale-Check (Validation)

Test an intervention across nested scales:

1. **Name the scales.** Which scales does this intervention touch? (personal, community, bioregional, national, planetary)
2. **Trace couplings.** How does the intervention at scale S_n affect S_n-1 (below) and S_n+1 (above)?
3. **Check for degenerative coupling.** Does optimising at one scale degrade another? If so, the intervention is not regenerative — it is merely locally sustainable.
4. **Seek regenerative coupling.** Can the intervention be redesigned so that health at one scale generates health at adjacent scales?

## §11. The And-Yet (Shadow)

### 11.1 Not Yet a Full Paradigm Shift

Taveras-Dalmau et al. (2025), in the most rigorous external assessment of the regenerative field (*Ambio*, Springer; 320 articles reviewed), found that regeneration meets *several but not all* criteria for a paradigm shift. It functions as a "potentially displacing paradigm" with significant gaps — particularly in connection to power, governance, and structural societal change. The regenerative paradigm is promising but incomplete.

### 11.2 The Inner-World Bias

The same review found that the first core principle ("inner world") appears in 86% of the regenerative literature. The field emphasises consciousness, worldview, and belief transformation while under-developing verifiable, structural system change. This is a manifestation of "paradigm blindness" within the regenerative paradigm itself — a tendency to mistake changed perception for changed conditions.

### 11.3 Corporate Co-option

"Regenerative" is being rapidly adopted by corporate actors — particularly in agriculture and fashion — without corresponding practice change. Without legal or regulatory definition, the term has no verification mechanism. This risks reducing "regenerative" to the fate of "sustainable" — a marketing term hollowed of meaning through promiscuous application.

### 11.4 Western-Centric Framing

Despite drawing on principles that resonate across cultures (reciprocity, place-adaptation, living-systems thinking), the framework's intellectual lineage is overwhelmingly Western European: Bateson, Capra, Maturana & Varela, Antonovsky, Wilber. Indigenous and non-Western knowledge systems that have practised regenerative principles for millennia are acknowledged but not centred.

### 11.5 Depoliticisation Risk

The focus on "crisis of perception" and "worldview transformation" can inadvertently depoliticise structural power asymmetries. If the root cause of ecological crisis is framed as epistemological (we see wrongly) rather than political-economic (power is distributed unjustly), then the prescribed response — changed perception — may leave extractive structures intact. Seeing clearly is necessary but not sufficient when the problem is also about who controls what.

### 11.6 The Gentrification Shadow

In urban contexts, particularly in the UK and US, "regeneration" has a specific and painful history as a euphemism for gentrification — the displacement of existing communities in the name of improvement. Wahl's usage is ecologically grounded and distinct from this urban-planning usage, but the terminological overlap is not innocent. Users of the framework should be aware of this shadow.

### 11.7 The Self-Referential Test

> *"If the regenerative framework were applied to itself, where would it fail?"*

A framework that advocates place-adapted, diverse, emergent responses — but is itself articulated as a single book by a single author from a particular cultural position — risks becoming the universal prescription it critiques. The questions-based methodology mitigates this (it opens rather than closes), but the framework's own institutional expression (conferences, certifications, consultancies) tends toward the standardisation it opposes. Regenerative thinking must regenerate itself — or it becomes sustainable-thinking-with-better-branding.
