## Attribution & Ownership

**The Wheel of Consent is a somatic consent framework developed by Betty Martin, D.C.** It emerged from Martin's observation of two independent dimensions in consensual touch — *who is doing* and *who it is for* — first noticed while practising the **3-Minute Game**, an exercise created by **Harry Faddis** in 1998 for a BDSM workshop at the Body Electric School.

Martin, a chiropractor with nearly 30 years of bodywork practice, formalised these observations into a four-quadrant model across the 2000s–2010s. She co-founded the **School of Consent** (2018) with **Robyn Dalzen** and published the framework's sole systematic account in 2021.

**Key Works:**

- Martin, B. & Dalzen, R. (2021). *The Art of Receiving and Giving: The Wheel of Consent*. Eugene, OR: Luminare Press. 422pp. ISBN 978-1-64388-308-3.
- Faddis, H. (1998). The 3-Minute Game. Body Electric School workshop exercise. (Documented via practitioner oral history; see Somatic Consent interview.)
- School of Consent (2018–present). Facilitator training curriculum. AASECT-approved Continuing Education Provider.

## §1. The Core Constructs

### 1.1 The Two Axes

The Wheel is built on two independent questions:

| Axis | Question | Values |
|------|----------|--------|
| **Action** | Who is doing? | I am doing / They are doing |
| **Gift** | Who is it for? | It is for me / It is for them |

These two binary axes are **independent** — knowing who is doing does not tell you who it is for. This independence is Martin's foundational observation and the framework's distinctive contribution.

### 1.2 The Four Quadrants

Crossing the two axes produces four distinct relational positions:

| Quadrant | I am... | It is for... | I ask... | The gift I give |
|----------|---------|-------------|----------|-----------------|
| **Serving** | Doing | Them | "May I...?" | My action |
| **Taking** | Doing | Me | "May I...?" | Their sensation |
| **Receiving** | Being done to | Me | "Will you...?" | My enjoyment |
| **Allowing** | Being done to | Them | "Will you...?" | My body/access |

### 1.3 The Two Agreements

The four quadrants pair into two reciprocal agreements:

```
Agreement 1: SERVING ↔ RECEIVING
  The Receiver directs ("a little to the left")
  The Server acts ("like this?")
  The gift flows from Server to Receiver

Agreement 2: TAKING ↔ ALLOWING
  The Taker directs ("I'd like to touch your hand")
  The Allower permits ("yes, you may")
  The gift flows from Allower to Taker
```

**Key insight:** In each agreement, one person directs and the other follows — but the directing person is different in each pair. In Serving/Receiving, the *Receiver* directs. In Taking/Allowing, the *Taker* directs. Understanding which agreement you are in determines who has navigational authority.

### 1.4 "Want To" vs. "Willing To"

Martin distinguishes sharply between:

| | "Want to" | "Willing to" |
|-|-----------|-------------|
| **Definition** | Genuine desire; something you would choose freely | Agreement within limits; you consent but would not initiate |
| **Function** | Identifies your quadrant (what do I *want*?) | Identifies your boundary (how far am I *willing* to go?) |
| **Risk when confused** | Saying "I want to" when you mean "I'm willing to" erodes self-knowledge | Treating "willing to" as if it were "want to" creates false intimacy |

This distinction maps to Faden & Beauchamp's (1986) concept of **autonomous authorisation vs. effective consent** — the difference between genuinely choosing and merely going along. Martin arrived at the same distinction inductively through bodywork practice rather than through bioethical theory.

### 1.5 The 3-Minute Game

The Wheel's primary pedagogical and experiential tool, created by Harry Faddis (1998):

```
Round 1:
  Person A asks: "What would you like me to do to you for three minutes?"
  Person B answers. (Person B is Receiving; Person A is Serving.)
  → A does what B asked, for three minutes.

Round 2:
  Person A asks: "What would you like to do to me for three minutes?"
  Person B answers. (Person B is Taking; Person A is Allowing.)
  → B does what they asked, for three minutes.

Then switch roles and repeat.
```

**What the game reveals:**
- Most people find one or two quadrants easy and one or two difficult
- The difficulty reveals where consent literacy needs development
- "I don't know what I want" is itself a diagnostic signal (Autonomy under-exercised)
- "I'd rather you decide" may indicate habitual Allowing disguised as generosity

### 1.6 Boundaries and Limits

Martin distinguishes between:

| Concept | Definition | Character |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| **Boundary** | The domain of one's sovereignty — the territory that is *mine* | Structural; largely stable |
| **Limit** | The current edge of willingness — how far I will go right now | Contextual; changeable; negotiable |

Consent operates at the level of *limits* (negotiated, revocable), grounded in the reality of *boundaries* (sovereign, non-negotiable).

## §2. The And-Yet (Shadow)

### 2.1 No Academic Validation

The Wheel of Consent has **no peer-reviewed empirical validation**. No controlled studies, no psychometric testing, no formal qualitative research published in academic venues. Martin's book is published by an independent press (Luminare Press). The framework's credibility rests on practitioner adoption, not scientific evidence.

**Honest assessment:** The academic ancestors (Faden & Beauchamp on informed consent, Deci & Ryan on autonomy, BDSM consent scholarship) share genuine conceptual DNA with the Wheel. But these are *reconstructed* connections — our analysis, not Martin's claimed lineage. She built the framework from practice, not from literature.

### 2.2 Sole-Source Authority

The Wheel of Consent is effectively a **single-author framework** with no independent scholarly verification. This creates a single point of failure for the source literature. If Martin's account of the framework's origins or mechanics is inaccurate, there is no independent academic record to check against.

**Mitigation:** The 3-Minute Game (Faddis, 1998) has independent provenance. The game exists regardless of Martin's interpretation. But the four-quadrant model and the theoretical architecture are hers alone.

### 2.3 Power-Asymmetric Contexts

The Wheel assumes a context of **rough equality** between participants. In contexts of significant power asymmetry — therapeutic relationships, teacher-student dynamics, employer-employee — the freedom to say "no" or to identify what one *wants* (vs. what one is *willing* to endure) is structurally compromised.

Martin addresses this partially (the framework was developed for professional bodywork contexts where power dynamics are always present). But the Wheel's elegant simplicity can mask the structural forces that prevent genuine consent from being available in the first place.

This echoes the critique of Nussbaum's benign objectification argument (see [OBJECTIFICATION.md §4.1](OBJECTIFICATION.md)): "benign" may be a luxury available only to those already empowered.

### 2.4 Cultural Specificity

The framework assumes:
- A cultural context where **asking for what you want** is possible (not all cultures value or permit this)
- Comfort with **naming desire** explicitly (some traditions treat desire as private or shameful)
- A **dyadic model** of consent negotiation (many relational/communal cultures operate differently)
- That **touch** is a primary domain of consent (some traditions foreground other domains: speech, attention, presence)

The Wheel is most legible within Western, individualist, post-sexual-revolution contexts. Its cross-cultural applicability is untested.

### 2.5 The Meta-Question

> *When does a consent framework become a script that replaces genuine encounter?*

The Wheel can become performative — partners running through the "correct" consent questions without genuine contact. When "May I...?" becomes ritual rather than inquiry, the framework performs consent without producing it. The toolkit's clarity can substitute for the messy, inarticulate process of actually *feeling* what you want.

**Mitigation:** Martin herself emphasises that the Wheel is diagnostic, not prescriptive. It helps you *notice* where you are, not dictate where you should be. But tools tend to become rules in the hands of anxious practitioners.

# Repository Integration

## What This Changes

The Wheel of Consent inverts the conventional understanding of consent:

| Common Understanding | Wheel of Consent |
|---------------------|------------------|
| Consent = saying yes or no | Consent = knowing what you want *before* you answer |
| Giving = generosity | Giving can be Serving (for them) or Taking (for you) — different quadrants |
| Receiving = passivity | Receiving is active — the Receiver directs |
| Allowing = consent | Allowing is one specific quadrant, not the whole of consent |
| The question is "Do you consent?" | The question is "Who is this for?" |
| Boundaries are walls | Boundaries are sovereign territory; limits are the negotiable edges |

## Integration with CARDS

The [CARDS](CARDS.md) framework ([Je'anna L Clements](CARDS.md)) maps to all four quadrants:

### Quadrant-to-CARDS Mapping

| Quadrant | Primary CARDS Served | How |
|----------|---------------------|-----|
| **Serving** | **C** + **R** | Competence in skilled giving; Relatedness through attentive service |
| **Taking** | **A** + **C** | Autonomy to know and pursue what you want; Competence in asking |
| **Receiving** | **A** + **D** | Autonomy to direct; Dignity in having your desires matter |
| **Allowing** | **A** + **D** + **S** | Autonomy to grant access; Dignity in the gift being recognised; Safety in knowing you can revoke |

### Dysfunction-to-CARDS Mapping

When a quadrant is misidentified or involuntary:

| Dysfunction | Quadrant Confusion | CARDS Impact |
|-------------|-------------------|--------------|
| "I'm giving" (but it's depleting me) | Serving without wanting to → involuntary Allowing | **A** red, **S** orange |
| "I'm receiving" (but I didn't ask for this) | Being done to without directing → involuntary Allowing | **A** red, **D** red |
| "I'm just being generous" (but I'm taking) | Taking disguised as Serving | Other's **D** red, **A** red |
| "I don't know what I want" | No access to Taking or Receiving | **A** orange, **C** red |

### Bidirectional Diagnostic

**Forward (Wheel → CARDS):** If a quadrant is occupied involuntarily, predict which CARDS will degrade.

**Reverse (CARDS → Wheel):** If CARDS degradation is observed, check which quadrant confusion might be present.

```
Diagnostic Protocol:
1. Observe CARDS state
2. If A is red → check: does this person know which quadrant they are in?
3. If D is red → check: is their gift being recognised? Are they being treated as Serving when they are Allowing?
4. If S is red → check: is "Allowing" genuinely voluntary, or is it compliance?
5. If C is red → check: "I don't know what I want" → Taking/Receiving capacity under-developed
6. If R is red → check: is connection happening through genuine agreement, or through unexamined Allowing?
```

## Integration with OBJECTIFICATION

The Wheel operationalises [Nussbaum's](OBJECTIFICATION.md) benign objectification conditions (§3.2):

### Benign Conditions ↔ Wheel Mechanics

| Nussbaum's Condition | Wheel Operationalisation |
|---------------------|--------------------------|
| **Mutual respect** | Both parties know which quadrant they are in |
| **Rough equality** | Both can access all four quadrants (not structurally locked into one) |
| **Consent** | The appropriate question is asked ("May I?" / "Will you?") and answered honestly |
| **Ongoing relationship** | The 3-Minute Game is played within relational context |
| **Symmetry** | Roles rotate — Server becomes Receiver, Taker becomes Allower |
| **Absence of coercion** | "Want to" is distinguished from "willing to"; exit remains live |

### The Autonomy Paradox (Shared)

Nussbaum's autonomy paradox (§3.5) — that choosing to surrender autonomy is an *exercise* of autonomy — maps directly to the **Allowing** quadrant. Allowing is the consensual gift of access. It is not passivity. It is sovereignty expressed as generosity.

```
Nussbaum: "Who holds the exit?" → determines benign vs. malignant
Wheel:    "Is this Allowing, or is this happening-to-me?" → same question, embodied
```

### Objectification Feature Check

| Objectification Feature | Wheel Diagnostic |
|------------------------|------------------|
| **Instrumentality** | Is the other person being Served (for them) or Used (for me, without their knowledge)? |
| **Denial of autonomy** | Can they access all four quadrants, or are they locked into Allowing? |
| **Inertness** | Is Receiving being confused with passivity? (Receiving is active — the Receiver directs.) |
| **Fungibility** | Is the specific person's desire being attended to, or would anyone do? |
| **Denial of subjectivity** | Is "What do you want?" genuinely asked, or is it rhetorical? |

## Integration with Multi-Partiality

The Wheel maps to [Boszormenyi-Nagy's](MULTI_PARTIALITY.md) give-take balance (§5):

### Give-Take ↔ Quadrant Mapping

| Nagy's Concept | Wheel Equivalent |
|---------------|------------------|
| **Giving** | Serving (giving action) and Allowing (giving access) |
| **Taking** | Taking (taking sensation) and Receiving (taking direction) |
| **Balanced ledger** | Both parties can occupy all four quadrants over time |
| **Over-giving** | Habitual Serving or Allowing without accessing Taking or Receiving |
| **Over-taking** | Habitual Taking without Serving or Allowing |
| **Parentification** | Child locked into Allowing; parent takes without asking |
| **Destructive entitlement** | Unacknowledged ledger debt → displaced Taking (taking from the wrong person) |

### Rejunction through the Wheel

Multi-partiality's rejunction process (§5) can be operationalised through Wheel awareness:

```
1. Acknowledge which quadrants each party has been occupying
2. Name the imbalance ("You've been Allowing; I've been Taking")
3. Negotiate future give-take ("What would you like to Receive?")
4. Mark the transition (new agreement = new quadrant)
```

### Strain Point

The Wheel is **dyadic** (two people, one agreement). Multi-partiality is **systemic** (multiple parties, absent stakeholders, intergenerational ledgers). The Wheel does not directly address the multi-party dimension of consent. The Multi-Party Encounter protocols bridge this gap operationally, but the theoretical reconciliation remains incomplete.

## Execution Routines

### Routine A: Diagnostic (Where Am I on the Wheel?)

**Use when:** You notice discomfort, confusion, or resentment in a relational or facilitative context.

```
1. PAUSE
   Notice the discomfort. Name it if possible.

2. LOCATE
   Ask: "Am I doing, or is something being done to me?"
   Ask: "Is this for me, or for them?"
   → Identify your quadrant.

3. CHECK VOLUNTARINESS
   Ask: "Do I WANT to be here, or am I WILLING but would rather not?"
   → If "willing but would rather not": you may be in involuntary Allowing.

4. CHECK AGREEMENT
   Ask: "Did we both agree on which quadrant we're in?"
   → Mismatched quadrants = the source of most relational friction.

5. RESPOND
   If involuntary → name it. "I notice I'm Allowing but I didn't choose this."
   If mismatched → clarify. "I thought I was Receiving, but you seem to be Taking."
   If voluntary and matched → continue.
```

### Routine B: The 3-Minute Game (Experiential)

**Use when:** Building consent literacy; exploring quadrant access; training or workshop context.

```
1. PAIR UP
   Two participants face each other.

2. ROUND 1 (Serving/Receiving)
   Person A asks: "What would you like me to do to you?"
   Person B answers with a specific, bounded request.
   A serves B's request for three minutes.
   Both notice: what is this like?

3. ROUND 2 (Taking/Allowing)
   Person A asks: "What would you like to do to me?"
   Person B answers with a specific, bounded request.
   B does what they asked for three minutes. A allows.
   Both notice: what is this like?

4. SWITCH
   Reverse roles. Repeat rounds 1 and 2.

5. DEBRIEF
   Which rounds were easy? Which were difficult?
   Could you identify what you wanted?
   Where did you notice "willing to" disguised as "want to"?
   Which quadrant felt most unfamiliar?
```

**Context note:** The 3-Minute Game is typically played with non-sexual, everyday touch (hand massage, shoulder rub) or even non-touch actions (reading aloud, drawing). The framework's power lies in the *structure of the question*, not the intensity of the contact.

### Routine C: Facilitation Check (For Encounter Hosts)

**Use when:** Facilitating multi-party encounters, studio sessions, or group work involving touch or proximity.

```
1. PRE-SESSION
   Ensure all participants understand the four quadrants.
   Establish that "Allowing" is always voluntary and revocable.
   Connect to existing protocols (heat-check signals, Quiet Tap-Out).

2. DURING SESSION
   Watch for involuntary Allowing (participant enduring rather than choosing).
   Watch for Taking disguised as Serving ("I'm doing this for you" when it's for me).
   Watch for Receiving collapse ("I don't know what I want — you decide").

3. INTERVENTION (if needed)
   "Which quadrant are you in right now?"
   "Is that where you want to be?"
   "What would you need to move to a different quadrant?"

4. POST-SESSION
   Debrief quadrant experience.
   Note any quadrant access patterns (individual or group).
   Update CONSENT_LEDGER if applicable.
```

## Why This Matters for the Repository

1. **Consent operationalised:** The Wheel moves consent from abstract principle (CARDS Autonomy axis) to embodied practice — two questions, four quadrants, immediately applicable
2. **Somatic gap filled:** No existing lib entry addresses consent in the domain of touch and embodied interaction. The Wheel fills this gap specifically.
3. **OBJECTIFICATION grounded:** Nussbaum's benign objectification conditions (§3.2) are theoretical. The Wheel operationalises them — "Who holds the exit?" becomes "Which quadrant are you in, and did you choose it?"
4. **Multi-Party Encounter support:** The encounter methodology's heat-check signals, Quiet Tap-Out, and contact-laundering guard are protocol elements. The Wheel provides the conceptual frame that makes those protocols coherent.
5. **Philosophy of Body instantiated:** "The body as sovereign boundary" is a philosophical commitment. The Wheel shows what that sovereignty looks like in practice — boundary vs. limit, want vs. willing, voluntary vs. involuntary.
6. **Give-take balance embodied:** Multi-partiality's ledger of merits and entitlements is relational. The Wheel shows how that ledger manifests in the specific domain of embodied interaction.
