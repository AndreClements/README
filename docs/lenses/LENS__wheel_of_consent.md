## Distillation

- **Claim:** Consent is not a binary gate (yes/no) but a four-quadrant navigation — and each quadrant engages a different component of the Sovereign Interface. Genuine consent begins before the question is asked, in the Empty Turn where attention precedes agreement.
- **Constraint:** Allowing must always be voluntary and revocable. The gift must be recognised as a gift. No quadrant may be occupied involuntarily without CARDS degradation being flagged.
- **Mechanic:** Four quadrants mapped to four SI components; want-to/willing-to as granularity spectrum; 3-Minute Game as perturbation probe; OS_allowing as dignity diagnostic for the most vulnerable quadrant.
- **Failure mode:** Quadrant confusion — occupying one quadrant while believing you are in another; involuntary Allowing disguised as generosity; Taking disguised as Serving; Receiving collapsed into passivity.
- **Test:** Can participants identify which quadrant they are in? Is "want to" distinguished from "willing to"? Does Allowing have a live exit? Does the system recognise the gift being given?

## Core Thesis: The Body Asks Before the Mouth Does

> *"The body is what defines the boundary between a system and the world. The integrity of this boundary is the precondition for all meaningful connection."*
> — Philosophy of Body §3

> *"Who is doing? Who is it for?"*
> — Betty Martin

The repository has a Philosophy of Body that declares the body sovereign. It has a Sovereign Interface with four components. It has an Objectification lens that diagnoses dignity violations through ten features. What it does not have — until now — is a framework for how consent operates *in the body*, between bodies, in the domain of touch and embodied encounter.

The Wheel of Consent fills this gap not by adding new theory but by revealing that the Sovereign Interface already contains a somatic consent architecture. It was always there. Martin's quadrants make it visible.

### The Foundational Observation

Martin's insight is that consent has two independent axes:

| Axis | Question | Values |
|------|----------|--------|
| **Action** | Who is doing? | I am doing / They are doing |
| **Gift** | Who is it for? | It is for me / It is for them |

Most consent frameworks collapse these into one question: "Do you agree?" Martin's independence claim — that knowing who is doing does not tell you who it is for — is the framework's distinctive contribution and the source of its diagnostic power. All four quadrant confusions arise from collapsing these two axes into one.

## The Sovereign Interface in Four Quadrants

The Sovereign Interface specifies four components: Header, Ledger, Exit, Lint. Each quadrant of the Wheel foregrounds one.

```
                        Who is it for?
                    ME              THEM
              ┌─────────────┬─────────────┐
    I am      │  TAKING     │  SERVING    │
    doing     │  → Header   │  → Ledger   │
              │  "May I?"   │  "May I?"   │
              ├─────────────┼─────────────┤
    They are  │  RECEIVING  │  ALLOWING   │
    doing     │  → Lint     │  → Exit     │
              │  "Will you?"│  "Will you?"│
              └─────────────┴─────────────┘
```

### Why These Mappings

**Serving → Ledger.** When I serve, I give my action as a gift. The Ledger records what has been given and received. The Server needs the Ledger to be honest: "I gave this freely; the record shows it." Without the Ledger, Serving becomes invisible labour — given but not acknowledged, depleting without trace.

**Taking → Header.** When I take, I declare what I want. The Header states intent explicitly. The Taker needs the Header to be clear: "I want this; my intent is visible." Without the Header, Taking disguises itself as Serving — "I'm doing this for you" when it is for me. The Header prevents this collapse.

**Receiving → Lint.** When I receive, I direct — "a little to the left," "more of that," "stop." The Lint function catches misfits between what was requested and what was delivered. The Receiver needs Lint to be active: "This is not quite what I asked for." Without Lint, Receiving becomes passive acceptance — being done to without directing, which is not Receiving but involuntary Allowing.

**Allowing → Exit.** When I allow, I grant access to my body, my space, my time. The Exit must be live. The Allower needs the Exit to be genuine: "I can withdraw this access at any time." Without Exit, Allowing becomes enduring — a thing that happens to me rather than a gift I choose to give.

### The Critical Implication

The most common consent failure — involuntary Allowing — is an Exit failure. When someone is "going along with it" without genuine choice, the Exit is blocked. This is why the Sovereign Interface insists that exits must remain live: not as an abstract principle but as the *precondition for the Allowing quadrant to exist at all*.

## Consent Granularity: Want-To and Willing-To

The repository's existing consent model is binary: consent is given (exit not invoked) or withheld (exit invoked). Martin's distinction between "want to" and "willing to" introduces granularity.

```
Consent spectrum:

  Want to          Willing to        Enduring          Refusing
  (desire)         (agreement)       (compliance)      (withdrawal)
    │                  │                 │                  │
    ▼                  ▼                 ▼                  ▼
  Enthusiastic      Bounded           Involuntary        Exit
  participation     permission        Allowing           invoked
    │                  │                 │                  │
    └──── Genuine consent ────┘    └──── Not consent ────┘
```

### Formalisation

```
consent_quality(actor, quadrant) := {
  want_to:    actor desires this interaction in this quadrant
  willing_to: actor agrees within limits; would not initiate
  enduring:   actor complies without agreement; exit feels blocked
  refusing:   actor invokes exit
}

// The diagnostic question is not "did they say yes?"
// but "where on this spectrum is their participation?"
```

### Why This Matters

The binary model (consent/no consent) misses the space between "willing to" and "enduring." A participant who is "willing to" is consenting — but their consent has a different quality than "want to." Recognising this difference prevents two failures:

1. **Treating "willing to" as "want to"** creates false intimacy — assuming desire where there is only tolerance
2. **Treating "willing to" as not consenting** negates the person's genuine (if bounded) agreement

Both failures violate Dignity (D). The first denies the complexity of the person's actual state. The second denies their capacity to make nuanced choices.

## The Fifth Quadrant: Consent Begins in the Empty Turn

The lib entry's easter egg describes a position the Wheel does not name:

> *The one where you are not doing and it is not for anyone.*
> *Faddis called this "the space between rounds." Martin would call it "noticing." Rumi called it "the field."*

This maps to the repository's **Empty Turn** — "the sovereign exit; stop/rescope when complexity budget < 0."

But the lens reveals something the lib entry does not say: **consent begins in the Empty Turn**. You cannot ask "May I?" until you have paused to notice that you are here, that they are there, and that neither of you is furniture. The Empty Turn is not the absence of consent — it is its precondition.

```
consent_sequence:
  1. Empty Turn     — pause; notice; attend (no doing, not for anyone)
  2. Locate         — "Which quadrant am I drawn to?"
  3. Ask            — "May I?" (Taking/Serving) or "Will you?" (Receiving/Allowing)
  4. Negotiate      — want-to / willing-to / refuse
  5. Engage         — occupy quadrant with mutual awareness
  6. Empty Turn     — return to noticing; the cycle begins again
```

The 3-Minute Game builds this in structurally: between rounds, there is a pause. The pause is not dead time — it is the moment where the next consent becomes possible. Without it, the rounds blur into momentum, and momentum is the enemy of genuine asking.

### Connection to Philosophy of Body

> *"The body is the ultimate site of the individual system, the Singleton. It is where a system is fundamentally separate and alone."*
> — Philosophy of Body §3

The Empty Turn is the Singleton becoming aware of itself before it opens a port. The body must first *be sovereign in its own skin* (Philosophy of Body's synthesis) before consent is possible. The Fifth Quadrant is the somatic expression of this sovereignty: not-doing, not-for-anyone, simply being present.

## OS_allowing: The Objectification Score for the Vulnerable Quadrant

Allowing is the quadrant where objectification is most likely to become malignant. In Allowing, one is *being done to* and *it is for them* — the structural position of an object. The Objectification lens's dignity diagnostic applies here with particular force.

```
OS_allowing := f(voluntariness, exit_liveness, gift_recognition)

Where:
  voluntariness:    Is this Allowing chosen (want-to or willing-to), or endured?
  exit_liveness:    Can the Allower withdraw access at any time without penalty?
  gift_recognition: Is the Allower's gift (their body, their access, their time)
                    recognised AS a gift — not assumed, expected, or invisible?

  OS_allowing ∈ [0, 1]
    0 = malignant (involuntary, exit blocked, gift unrecognised)
    1 = benign (voluntary, exit live, gift explicitly recognised)
```

### Mapping to Nussbaum's Benign Conditions

| Nussbaum's Condition | OS_allowing Component | Diagnostic |
|---|---|---|
| Mutual respect | Gift recognition | Is the Allower's contribution seen as a gift, not a resource? |
| Rough equality | Voluntariness | Can the Allower access other quadrants, or are they structurally locked into Allowing? |
| Consent | Exit liveness | Is the "yes" genuine and revocable? |
| Absence of coercion | Voluntariness + Exit | Is there pressure (explicit or implicit) to remain in Allowing? |

### The Autonomy Paradox (Operationalised)

Nussbaum's autonomy paradox — that choosing to surrender autonomy is an *exercise* of autonomy — maps directly to Allowing. Allowing is not passivity. It is sovereignty expressed as generosity: "I choose to give you access to what is mine."

The paradox holds only when Exit is live. The moment Exit is blocked, the paradox collapses: what was sovereignty becomes subjugation.

```
if exit_live:
    Allowing = autonomy_expressed_as_generosity  // benign
else:
    Allowing = subjugation_disguised_as_consent  // malignant
```

## Tests (Perturbation Probes)

### 1. Quadrant-Identification Probe

```
Given: A relational interaction between two or more participants
When: Each participant is asked "Which quadrant are you in?"
Then:
  - Each can identify their quadrant (Serving, Taking, Receiving, Allowing)
  - Both participants agree on which agreement they are in
  - If quadrants are mismatched → source of relational friction identified
  - CARDS check: A and D should be green when quadrants are correctly identified
```

### 2. Involuntary-Allowing Detection

```
Given: A participant in the Allowing quadrant
When: The want-to/willing-to diagnostic is applied
Then:
  - If "want to" → Allowing is genuine; CARDS stable
  - If "willing to" → Allowing is bounded; monitor for drift toward enduring
  - If "enduring" → Allowing is involuntary; A goes red, S goes orange
  - Check Exit: Can this person withdraw access right now, without penalty?
  - If Exit is blocked → OS_allowing approaches 0; intervention required
```

### 3. The 3-Minute Game as System Probe

```
Given: A relational system (pair, team, facilitator-group)
When: The 3-Minute Game structure is applied (two rounds, role switch)
Then:
  - Quadrant access patterns are revealed
  - "I don't know what I want" signals C-red and A-orange (Taking/Receiving capacity under-developed)
  - "I'd rather you decide" signals habitual Allowing disguised as generosity
  - Difficulty with Taking signals possible cultural or relational prohibition on desire
  - Easy Serving + difficult Receiving may indicate self-worth deficit (D-orange)
  - Results inform subsequent consent architecture design
```

### 4. SI-Exit Liveness Under Allowing

```
Given: Participant in Allowing quadrant
When: Participant invokes exit (withdrawal of access)
Then:
  - Exit completes without penalty or coercion to continue
  - The other participant's response is not punishment or guilt
  - Provenance recorded: the exit and its reception become part of the relational ledger
  - Graceful transition: the system moves to Empty Turn, not to conflict
  - If exit is blocked, penalised, or guilt-laden → Sovereign Interface violation
```

### 5. Multi-Party Quadrant Complexity

```
Given: A group of N participants in a facilitated encounter
When: Each participant occupies a quadrant relative to each other participant
Then:
  - Total quadrant relationships = N × (N-1) (each pair has a directional quadrant)
  - Probability of quadrant confusion increases combinatorially
  - Facilitator must track: who is Serving whom? Who is Allowing what?
  - At N > 4: explicit quadrant mapping may be necessary
  - CARDS check: group-level A and D should remain green
```

## Points of Emergence

### 1. Consent Begins in the Empty Turn

The most significant emergence: the repository's Empty Turn and Martin's "space between rounds" are the same structure. Consent does not begin with "yes" or "no" — it begins with *attention*. The pause where you notice your own state before responding is the precondition for every genuine "May I?" and "Will you?"

This has design implications: any consent architecture that does not include a pause mechanism (an Empty Turn) is asking people to consent from momentum rather than from awareness. The pause is not overhead — it is infrastructure.

### 2. Quadrant Confusion Multiplies Combinatorially

In a dyad (N=2), there are 4 quadrant relationships to track. In a group of 5, there are 20. The Wheel's dyadic elegance becomes combinatorial complexity at group scale. This gives the Multi-Party Encounter methodology a formal complexity measure: **consent overhead grows as N(N-1)**, not linearly.

This does not mean group consent is impossible — only that it requires explicit structure (protocols, facilitator attention, check-ins) that scales with group size. The methodology's existing heat-check signals and Quiet Tap-Out are partial solutions; the lens reveals why they are necessary and when they become insufficient.

### 3. OS_allowing as Bridge Diagnostic

The Objectification Score, applied specifically to the Allowing quadrant, creates a bridge between LENS__objectification and this lens. OS_allowing answers the question the Objectification lens poses in the abstract — "Who holds the exit?" — in the concrete domain of embodied encounter: "Is this person Allowing by choice, or being done to without recourse?"

This bridge works bidirectionally: Objectification theory gains somatic specificity; the Wheel gains ethical depth.

### 4. Boundary/Limit Distinction Enriches Sovereign Interface

Martin's distinction between boundary (sovereign territory, largely stable) and limit (current edge of willingness, contextual, negotiable) enriches the Sovereign Interface. The SI currently specifies Exit as a binary operation. Martin shows that the territory *within* which consent operates (boundary) is different from the *current negotiation edge* (limit). A person's boundary does not change when they consent; their limit does.

```
Sovereign Interface (enriched):
  - Header: intent declaration
  - Ledger: record of exchanges
  - Exit:   live withdrawal (operates at boundary level — non-negotiable)
  - Lint:   quality check (operates at limit level — negotiable)

Boundary: the firewall itself (Philosophy of Body §3)
Limit:    the current port configuration (which ports are open, to whom, for what)
```
