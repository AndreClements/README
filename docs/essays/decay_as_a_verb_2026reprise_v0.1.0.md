# Urban Decay is a Verb
*Herri expansion draft v0.1.0 - 2026 reprise*

The garbage trucks stopped coming, and when they came back the whistles were beautiful. Nothing about the trucks had changed.

That is the first trap in the word decay: it pretends the thing itself has failed.

The truck is still a truck. The street is still a street. A staircase still knows the shape of a body climbing. A city does not become less material when care withdraws from it. If anything, it becomes more material: concrete without the promise, road without maintenance, glass without regard, refuse without the small municipal choreography that used to make it disappear by morning.

And then the trucks return, and the whistles become beautiful.

Not because refuse collection is beautiful, exactly. Because a relation has returned. A person in a cab, a route, a wage, diesel, a bin lifted and emptied, a street allowed not to drown in its own remainder. The whistle is not decoration. It is a field arriving in sound.

Still.

A pedestrian staircase smoulders by itself on a Sunday morning, shot through a car window. The window stays in the picture. (The window was there because three months earlier the photographer went in on foot and was beaten for the camera. One night is not a life; the sentence stops there.)

Not the staircase burning. Cable burning under it: insulation cooked off for the copper. The smell is what tells you, and only if you have lived there.

The photograph knows less than the body did. The frame gives concrete, smoke, bright noon, a thin flare at the top edge where the car window catches itself in the act. It shows the stair as a scarred geometry, blackened walls, the green pushing down from above, and some small red thing in the dust that insists on being seen without becoming meaningful. It does not show the smell. It does not show the economy of the few hundred rand. It does not show the hands that stripped the cable, or why they needed to, or who owned the copper before ownership became a verb with a match in it.

So the caption has to carry what the image cannot.

Decay is not a noun, and the verb has a subject.

Some subjects are easy to name because they arrive wearing municipal logos, development plans, police uniforms, quarterly reports. Some are harder because they arrive as need. A person burning cable for copper is not the same subject as the field that made copper worth burning and a city worth abandoning. To collapse them is dishonest. To pretend they do not touch is also dishonest.

En tog.

The argument runs backwards in a gully: from cable to copper, from copper to cash, from cash to food or smoke or school shoes or debt or a body held together for one more day. Configuration is dismantled to liberate substance. That is one species of decay. Not entropy alone, not weather alone, not the romance of peeling paint under soft light. Harvest. Extraction after extraction. The mine becomes the city; the city becomes a mine.

Johannesburg should not be surprised by this. The gold left long ago and the streets still bend around where it was.

A city's mass is banked relation: grids, girders, 4am shifts poured as concrete. It is mine dust and hostel corridors, tramlines remembered in street widths, small shops pressed into old facades, someone selling fruit where capital no longer sees a tenant, someone sleeping in a doorway where the doorway still performs shelter. Decay is not the mass leaving. It is the field withdrawing: capital, concern, the weighing, while the structure stands like a score nobody sings.

Mass that persists can always be re-weighed, never only on the original owner's scale.

This matters because ruins lie beautifully.

They lie even when the photographer does not mean them to. They lie by letting form survive context. A burnt truck at night becomes a heroic carcass, all gleam and black ribs under a sodium sky. A broken wall in Bordeaux becomes patina. A derelict railway carriage becomes a long beige animal resting beside weeds. A warehouse with LIFE painted high on the far wall becomes almost too generous a sentence. The camera loves surfaces that have suffered. It loves them partly because suffering makes texture.

There is no clean position here for a photographer. Not really.

I generally avoid making identifiable people carry the spectacle of decay. This is a constraint, not innocence. It can protect dignity in one frame and erase agency in the next. It can refuse to benefit from another person's misfortune and still benefit from the scene that misfortune helped make visible. It can keep a child out of an argument and thereby keep childhood out of the city the argument claims to read. The rule is too blunt. The danger is not.

Dignity is the thing.

Not absence. Not purity. Not the clean empty ruin where nobody has to answer for the looking. Dignity means asking what a photograph asks of the person inside it, and what it gives back, and whether the answer changes when the person is not pictured but remains causal. Dignity means admitting that a car window is sometimes cowardice and sometimes prudence and sometimes the only honest edge the picture has. Dignity means refusing the little imperial thrill of access: I was there; I saw; I brought back proof.

Proof for whom?

The people living with decay seldom need proof that it exists. They need collection, repair, light, water, safety, work, rest. They need not to become evidence in someone else's field of concern. And yet the image can still matter because the field is uneven. Some rooms have to be shown what other rooms already breathe.

That is the narrow path: not to make suffering ornamental, not to keep one's hands clean by refusing to look, not to confuse one's own risk with the city's, not to turn caution into virtue. To look, and declare the toll. To frame, and leave the frame visible.

The window stays in the picture.

There are other images in the folder. Diepsloot: fire low in a drainage line, smoke rising through branches, a streetlight making an altar by accident. Durban Deep: a ruin held between walls and leaves, all the old extraction softened by green, which is not forgiveness. Standerton: a railway carriage with its windows broken, roof flaking under electric yellow, the name of a town still painted on the side like an address nobody will deliver to. Hillbrow: a school sign, a camera on the pavement, a tower of lit windows behind it. Zandspruit: children and a ball, smoke in the background, life in the foreground, and the caption must not steal either.

Life keeps entering the frame without permission.

This is why decay as a noun is too weak. The noun lets us sigh at surfaces. The verb asks who is doing what to whom, and what remains possible after the doing. It asks why one city service can vanish until its return sounds like grace. It asks why copper weighs more outside the wall than inside the cable. It asks how a road can carry traffic, protest, refuse, play, smoke, and memory without any one of them owning the meaning of the road.

It asks what we are weighing.

The old scale says: property value, service delivery, urban management, investment confidence, crime statistics, heritage risk. These are not useless. They are only not enough. Another scale reads relation: who still gathers, who still repairs, who still walks, who still whistles from a truck before sunrise. Another reads refusal: what the city has stopped giving, what people take back, what gets burned because burning is the available converter. Another reads beauty and finds itself in trouble.

The trouble is useful if it does not get converted too quickly into style.

I do not want the burnt staircase to become a handsome thing. It is handsome, which is part of the problem. I do not want the smoke to become atmosphere. It was atmosphere, in the most literal sense, entering lungs. I do not want the green at the top of the frame to become hope. It might be hope. It might also be neglect with chlorophyll. More than one centre, more than one path.

The city is not dead. It is not alive in the simple congratulatory sense either. It is being acted upon, and acting back, and being harvested, and reconfigured, and weighed by fields that do not agree. A ruin can be a wound, a shelter, a resource, a danger, a playground, a studio, a market, an archive, a warning, a home. The same mass, different fields. The same kilogram on the moon, and nothing about the kilogram changed.

But a city is not a kilogram. Its fields are made by us, which means they can be withdrawn by us, and returned by us, and fought over by us. Concern is not weather after all, though it behaves like weather when you are caught inside it. It has budgets. It has names. It has hands.

The garbage trucks stopped coming, and when they came back the whistles were beautiful.

Nothing about the trucks had changed.

Everything about the field had.

Still.
