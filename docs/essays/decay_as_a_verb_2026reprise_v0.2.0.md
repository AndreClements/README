# Urban Decay is a Verb
*Herri expansion draft v0.2.0 · 2026 reprise · 2026-07-26. Grown from the seed (v0.0.3) and the v0.1.0 draft; the Zandspruit crossing now worked in the body.*

The garbage trucks stopped coming, and when they came back the whistles were beautiful. Nothing about the trucks had changed.

That is the first trap in the word decay: it pretends the thing itself has failed.

The truck is still a truck. The street is still a street. A staircase still knows the shape of a body climbing. A city does not become less material when care withdraws from it. If anything it becomes more material: concrete without the promise, road without maintenance, glass without regard, refuse without the small municipal choreography that used to make it disappear by morning.

And then the trucks return, and the whistles become beautiful. A relation has come back: a person in a cab, a route, a wage, diesel, a bin lifted and emptied, a street allowed not to drown in its own remainder. The whistle is not decoration. It is a field arriving in sound.

Still.

A pedestrian staircase smoulders by itself on a Sunday morning, shot through a car window. The window stays in the picture. (The window was there because three months earlier the photographer went in on foot and was beaten for the camera. One night is not a life; the sentence stops there.)

Not the staircase burning. Cable burning under it: insulation cooked off for the copper. The smell is what tells you, and only if you have lived there.

The photograph knows less than the body did. The frame gives concrete, smoke, bright noon, the stair as scarred geometry, green pushing down over the top edge, some small red thing in the dust that insists on being seen without becoming meaningful, and a thin flare where the car window catches itself in the act. It does not give the smell. It does not give the economy of a few hundred rand, or the hands that stripped the cable, or why they needed to, or who owned the copper before ownership became a verb with a match in it.

So the caption has to carry what the image cannot.

Decay is not a noun, and the verb has a subject.

Some subjects are easy to name because they arrive wearing municipal logos, development plans, police uniforms, quarterly reports. Some are harder because they arrive as need. A person burning cable for copper is not the same subject as the field that made copper worth burning and a city worth abandoning. To collapse them is dishonest. To pretend they do not touch is also dishonest.

En tog.

The argument runs backwards in a gully: from cable to copper, from copper to cash, from cash to food or smoke or school shoes or debt or a body held together for one more day. Configuration is dismantled to liberate substance. That is one species of decay. Not entropy, not weather, not the romance of peeling paint under soft light. Harvest. The mine becomes the city, and the city becomes a mine.

Johannesburg should not be surprised by this. The gold left long ago and the streets still bend around where it was.

A city's mass is banked relation: grids, girders, 4am shifts poured as concrete, tramlines remembered in street widths, small shops pressed into old facades, someone selling fruit where capital no longer sees a tenant, someone sleeping in a doorway where the doorway still performs shelter. Decay is not the mass leaving. It is the field withdrawing (capital, concern, the weighing) while the structure stands like a score nobody sings.

Mass that persists can always be re-weighed, never only on the original owner's scale.

This matters because ruins lie beautifully.

They lie even when the photographer does not mean them to. They lie by letting form survive context. A broken wall in Bordeaux becomes patina. A derelict carriage becomes a long beige animal resting beside weeds. A warehouse with LIFE painted high on the far wall becomes almost too generous a sentence. The camera loves surfaces that have suffered. It loves them partly because suffering makes texture.

There is no clean position here for a photographer. Not really.

Zandspruit, 2012. A road closed by a barricade, and because it is closed it is a pitch: two boys and a ball on the best open tar in the district that afternoon. Fire low across the lane behind them, smoke drifting into the trees, a crowd along the fence going about the business of being a crowd. The ball is mid-roll. The younger boy is following it the way you follow a thing you are about to be responsible for. The older one has turned, and is looking straight down the lens.

He is identifiable. I will not pretend that is a small thing.

The practice usually avoids making identifiable people carry the spectacle of decay. Usually: not always, and not proudly. It is a constraint, not innocence. Benefiting from another's misfortune is a real danger (the frame that converts somebody's worst week into my good year), and a child cannot audit that bargain, and the picture outlives the afternoon by decades.

En tog. Avoidance is also an omission. Keep the child out of the argument and you keep childhood out of the city the argument claims to read: the clean unpeopled stair, the empty ruin, a Johannesburg in which nobody plays. That is not a more ethical city. It is only a quieter picture. The rule protects dignity in one frame and erases agency in the next, and I have written it down, before, more absolutely than I hold it.

Context matters. These boys are not a misfortune I am harvesting. The barricade is behind them and it is not on them: the road did not become a pitch despite the trouble, it became a pitch because of it, and the taking of it is theirs. The play is in front of the argument, not inside it. The resilience in the frame belongs to them, and I would rather it were seen than not.

And yet context is what a photograph sheds first. Captioned, he is a boy playing ball on a closed road. Uncaptioned, in some other room, he is evidence: of decay, of danger, of whatever that room was already inclined to see. I cannot follow the picture into every room it will enter. I can only load it before it goes.

Dignity is the governing concern. Not absence. Not purity. Dignity asks what a photograph asks of the person inside it, and what it gives back, and whether he would recognise himself in the sentence I have just written (a young man now, somewhere, who spent one afternoon owning a road). The question does not close, and I am leaving it open on purpose, in print, where it can be checked.

There are other rooms in the folder. Diepsloot: refuse fire low in a drainage line at night, smoke rising through branches, the streetlight above it working perfectly. Durban Deep: mining afterlife held between walls and leaves, all the old extraction softened by green, which is not forgiveness. Standerton: a carriage stripped to its ribs on a dead siding, its rule still stencilled on the flank, outliving the institution that meant it. Hillbrow: my camera on its tripod on the pavement, screen lit, under a school's signboard with an abortion number pasted to the pole below (I did not compose that; the street had already written the caption). And LIFE, high on its wall across the fire escapes, almost let in as a punchline and kept, instead, as a counterweight.

Life keeps entering the frame without permission.

I do not want the burnt staircase to become a handsome thing. It is handsome, which is part of the problem. I do not want the smoke to become atmosphere. It was atmosphere, in the most literal sense, entering lungs. I do not want the green at the top of the frame to become hope. It might be hope. It might also be neglect with chlorophyll. More than one centre, more than one path.

This is why decay as a noun is too weak. The noun lets us sigh at surfaces. The verb asks who is doing what to whom, and what remains possible after the doing. It asks why one city service can vanish until its return sounds like grace. It asks why copper weighs more outside the wall than inside the cable. It asks how a road can carry traffic, protest, refuse, play, smoke and memory without any one of them owning the meaning of the road.

It asks what we are weighing.

The old scale reads: property value, service delivery, investment confidence, crime statistics, heritage risk. Not useless. Only not enough. Another scale reads relation: who still gathers, who still repairs, who still walks, who still whistles from a truck before sunrise. Another reads refusal: what the city has stopped giving, what people take back, what gets burned because burning is the available converter. Another reads beauty, and finds itself in trouble, and the trouble is useful for exactly as long as it resists becoming style.

The city is not dead. It is not alive in the simple congratulatory sense either. It is being acted upon, and acting back, and being harvested, and reconfigured, and weighed by fields that do not agree. A ruin can be a wound, a shelter, a resource, a danger, a playground, a studio, a market, an archive, a warning, a home. The same structure, weighed in rooms that will never agree on it.

Concern is not weather after all, though it behaves like weather when you are caught inside it. It has budgets. It has names. It has hands. A field withdrawn by us can be returned by us, and fought over by us, and the fight is the city.

The garbage trucks stopped coming, and when they came back the whistles were beautiful.

Nothing about the trucks had changed.

Everything about the field had.

Still.

Caption ethics: the practice usually avoids making identifiable people carry the spectacle of decay. That is not a clean defence, only a constraint under pressure, and the Zandspruit crossing is worked in the body of the essay rather than settled by a rule. Dignity, not absence, is the standard.

Published only with the essay's Zandspruit passage standing beside it (the caption and the body carry the context the frame will shed).
