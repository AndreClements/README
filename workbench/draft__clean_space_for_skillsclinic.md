## The Short Version

Elana, there's a facilitation method called **Clean Space** that I think slots neatly into a gap in your Presentation Clinic work — specifically with the technical clients (engineers, developers, architects, analysts) who *know their material deeply* but can't translate it into a presentation structure that an audience can follow.

You already know the problem: these clients think in abstractions, logic chains, system relationships. When you say "let's structure your presentation," they can tell you everything — but not in order, and not in a way that maps to slides or a narrative arc. When you say "sketch it on a whiteboard," some of them freeze because they don't think visually.

Clean Space gets around this by using **physical movement** instead of visual thinking. The client walks around a room, stands in different spots, and reports what they know "from here." Each spot becomes a Post-It note on the floor. After enough spots exist, the spatial arrangement *is* the structure — and the client built it by walking it, not drawing it.

You'll recognise the lineage. David Grove (the developer) trained in NLP in the late 1970s, worked with Bandler and Grinder, then broke away in 1981 to develop his own approach. Clean Language came first (1980s) — minimal-projection questioning using the client's exact words. Clean Space came later (2000s) — same principles, but the exploration happens in physical space rather than verbal metaphor.

The NLP connection means the conceptual territory won't be foreign. But the execution is different enough to be genuinely new.

## What Clean Space Is

A facilitation process where:

1. The client writes their topic on a piece of paper and places it somewhere in the room
2. The facilitator says: **"Find a space that knows something about [topic]"**
3. The client walks to a spot and stands there
4. The facilitator asks: **"And what do you know... from here?"**
5. The client speaks — whatever comes
6. Facilitator: **"Write that down"** — client puts key words on a Post-It, places it on the floor
7. **"And is there another space that knows something?"** — client moves to a new spot
8. Repeat until the room is populated with Post-It notes
9. At each new space, also ask: **"And what do you know from here about [that other space]?"** — this surfaces relationships between the parts
10. Once enough spaces exist (typically 5–15), **emergent properties** appear — patterns, structures, connections the client didn't see before

The facilitator uses only **6 questions and 4 directing statements**. That's the whole toolkit. The simplicity is the point: the less the facilitator says, the more the client's own structure emerges.

### The 6 Questions
- "And what do you know... from here?"
- "And is there anything else you know... from here?"
- "And what do you know from here about [space X]?"
- "And what kind of space is this space?"
- "And is there a space that knows something about [X]?"
- "And what do you know now?"

### The 4 Directing Statements
- "Find a space that knows something about [X]"
- "Write that down"
- "Go to [space X]"
- "Find a space that knows something else"

## Why This Fits the Presentation Clinic

### The problem Clean Space solves that your existing tools may not

Your Presentation Clinic already covers messaging, story structure, delivery, and audience psychology. Clean Space doesn't replace any of that. It addresses a specific upstream bottleneck:

**The client who can't get their material *out of their head and into a structure* in the first place.**

This is the person who:
- Knows their topic deeply but explains it in a stream of consciousness
- Can answer any question about their domain but can't organise it into a sequence
- Gets stuck when asked "what's the one thing you want the audience to take away?"
- Says "it's all connected" and means it — they can't separate the parts because in their mental model, everything depends on everything else
- Thinks predominantly in **auditory/logical** channels — internal monologue, if-then chains, system relationships — not in pictures or spatial arrangements

For this person, "let's outline your presentation" is premature. They don't know what the outline is yet. They know the *material*, but not the *shape*.

Clean Space gives them the shape by having them walk it.

### How it integrates with your existing workflow

```
BEFORE (current gap):
  Client arrives with deep expertise, no structure
  → You try to extract structure through conversation
  → Works sometimes; frustrating when client can't separate/sequence
  → Time-consuming; you're doing the structuring work

AFTER (with Clean Space inserted):
  Client arrives with deep expertise, no structure
  → You run a 30-60 minute Clean Space session
  → Client walks their own thinking; Post-Its on the floor
  → Client discovers their own structure (sequence, relationships, entry point)
  → NOW your existing Presentation Clinic tools apply:
     messaging, story arc, slides, delivery coaching
  → But the foundation is the client's own structure, not yours
```

Clean Space slots in as a **pre-structuring** step. It's not a replacement for your presentation methodology — it's the thing that happens *before* your methodology, for the clients who need it.

### Why this matters for credibility with technical clients

Technical people — especially engineers and developers — are wary of being told how to think. They've sat through corporate presentation training before and found it patronising. "Tell a story!" "Open with a hook!" "Use the rule of three!" These are valid techniques, but they feel imposed from outside.

Clean Space disarms that resistance because:
- **The facilitator doesn't provide content or structure** — the client discovers it
- **There's nothing to perform** — you just walk and talk
- **The result is genuinely theirs** — no template, no formula, no "presentation framework"
- **It works through their strength** (knowing their material) rather than demanding their weakness (visual/narrative organisation)

A developer who'd resist "let's storyboard your talk" will often engage willingly with "find a space that knows something about your architecture." It's concrete, it's exploratory, and it doesn't ask them to be someone they're not.

## What You'd Need to Try This

### Minimum viable experiment

You could trial this with a willing technical client in a single session:

**Setup:**
- A room with enough space to walk around (your training room works fine)
- Post-It notes and a pen
- 45–60 minutes

**Your role:**
- Ask the 6 questions and use the 4 directing statements — nothing else
- Don't interpret, suggest, or reorganise
- Don't say "that's great!" or "interesting!" — stay clean
- Write down the sequence of spaces the client visits (this becomes the narrative arc)

**The hardest part for you:**
Honestly? Shutting up. You're an experienced facilitator with 30 years of pattern recognition. You'll *see* the presentation structure before the client does. The discipline of Clean Space is to let them find it themselves. Your NLP training will help — you understand rapport and calibration — but the Clean stance is more austere than NLP. No leading, no pacing-and-leading, no reframing. Just reflecting and asking.

**After the session:**
The client will have Post-It notes on the floor in an arrangement. Photograph it. Then ask them to walk through the spaces in the order they'd present them. That walk-through is the first draft of their presentation structure.

*Now* your Presentation Clinic skills apply. Story arc, messaging, slide design, delivery — all of it. But built on a foundation the client owns.

### Learning the method properly

If you want to go deeper:
- **Book:** *Clean Language: Revealing Metaphors and Opening Minds* by Wendy Sullivan & Judy Rees (2008) — the most accessible introduction. You'll read it in a day.
- **Book:** *From Contempt to Curiosity* by Caitlin Walker (2014) — group applications; case studies from IT and business contexts. Directly relevant to your organisational work.
- **Training:** Clean Learning (cleanlearning.co.uk) offers Clean Space courses
- **The NLP bridge:** David Grove studied with Bandler and Grinder in 1978, became an NLP trainer, then left NLP in 1981. The Clean approach is what he built after NLP — keeping the precision, dropping the leading. Your NLP background means you'll learn this fast, but you'll also need to *unlearn* some NLP habits (especially pacing-and-leading and reframing — both are "unclean").

## What This Is Not

To be honest about the boundaries:

- **Clean Space is not a presentation methodology.** It's a pre-structuring tool. It gets the material out and arranged. The presentation work still needs your existing skills.
- **Not every client needs this.** Some people arrive with a clear structure and just need help with delivery or slides. Clean Space is for the ones who are genuinely stuck at the *what do I say and in what order* stage.
- **It's not therapy.** Grove developed Clean Language for trauma work, but Clean Space as used here is purely cognitive/organisational. You're not going into emotional territory — you're helping someone map their expertise.
- **The evidence base is practitioner-level, not clinical-trial level.** Clean Language has growing academic validation (British Journal of Management, 2014). Clean Space specifically has less — it's documented primarily by Lawley & Tompkins (practitioners, not academics). It works; the evidence for *why* it works is theoretical rather than experimental.
- **It requires practice.** The six questions look trivially simple. They are not. Asking "what do you know from here?" without adding "about your architecture" or "regarding the problem" is harder than it sounds. Every extra word is facilitator contamination.

## The Auditory-to-Spatial Bridge

Here's the specific mechanism for your "nerds who think in auditory/logical mode":

A client who thinks auditorily has rich internal monologue, if-then chains, verbal reasoning. They can *talk* about their topic at length. What they can't do is *see* it — and a presentation ultimately needs to be seen (by the audience, as slides; by the presenter, as structure).

Clean Space doesn't ask them to switch from auditory to visual. It routes through **kinesthetic/spatial**:

```
Auditory thinking (internal monologue, verbal reasoning)
    ↓ Clean Space bridges via ↓
Kinesthetic/spatial (walking, standing, physical position)
    ↓ naturally produces ↓
Visual/structural (Post-It notes on a floor = a visible map)
```

The client never has to "visualise." They just walk and talk — which is exactly what auditory thinkers are good at. The spatial arrangement happens as a side effect of movement, not as a demanded cognitive skill. And once the Post-Its are on the floor, the structure is visible whether or not the client is "a visual thinker."

This is the key insight: **you don't need to be a visual thinker to produce a visual output, if the process routes through a modality you're already strong in.**

## Possible Integration with Claude

You mentioned you use Claude in your work. A few thoughts:

### Where Claude helps with Clean Space
- **After the physical session:** The client photographs their Post-It arrangement. You can describe the spatial layout to Claude and ask it to help identify narrative arcs, missing transitions, or audience-facing framings. Claude won't have been in the room, but it can work with the *output* of the session.
- **Pre-session scoping:** If the client sends you their topic in advance, Claude can help you anticipate what domains might emerge as spaces — not to predetermine them, but so you're not blindsided by unfamiliar jargon.
- **Slide scaffolding:** Once the client's structure is clear (from the Clean Space session), Claude can help draft slide outlines, speaker notes, or visual metaphors based on the structure the client discovered.

### Where Claude does NOT help
- **During the session itself.** Clean Space is embodied — it requires physical presence, movement, and real-time calibration. No AI tool replaces that.
- **Replacing the facilitation.** Claude can ask Clean questions in text, but Clean Space's power comes from the physical movement and spatial cognition. A text-based version loses the mechanism that makes it work.
